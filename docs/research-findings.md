# Research findings

Six research lenses gathered for the Issho redesign. Each finding has an evidence grade (strong, moderate, weak or practitioner), details and caveats, what it means for the app, and its sources.

## How memory and practice research applies to Issho's daily structure for learning L2 vocabulary and kanji. Topics: retrieval practice (production vs recognition), spacing and intervals, FSRS vs SM-2 and the desired-retention trade-off, interleaving vs blocking, desirable difficulties, feedback content and timing, successive relearning, overlearning and repetitions within a session, pretesting and generation, sleep and consolidation, metacognitive illusions, and new items per day and workload. Method note: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through. After that, I checked citations by fetching DOI-specific records (OpenAlex, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar), publisher and author pages, and abstracts. Where a number comes only from a secondary summary, the finding says so. Evidence is mostly lab paired-associate studies, which are close to flashcard learning. A few classroom studies and large app datasets are included. I found no controlled study of kanji handwriting schedules specifically, so the handwriting implications are inferences from the general effects.

### Retrieval practice (trying to recall, then getting feedback) gives clearly better long-term retention than restudying. The effect is medium-sized and holds in real classrooms as well as labs.

**Evidence:** strong

Rowland (2014) meta-analysis: about 61 studies and 159 effect sizes, overall g ≈ 0.50 (count and g are from a secondary summary). Initial recall tests produced bigger benefits than recognition tests. Adesope et al. (2017, 118 studies): practice tests beat restudy and every other comparison condition. Benefits held for both retention and transfer, were about equal in classrooms and labs, and were larger when the final test came more than 1 day later. Mixed-format practice was strongest, and feedback added only slightly (per the Learning Scientists summary). Yang et al. (2021): 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.499. Moderators included test-format consistency, matching material, corrective feedback and number of test repetitions. Boundary: much of this evidence uses facts and prose, but L2 word-pair studies show the same pattern (e.g., Karpicke & Roediger 2008, Swahili–English).

**For Issho:** After the first exposure, every contact with an item should be a recall attempt, not a re-display. In the i+1 reading block, have learners try to read each sentence aloud before the audio plays (read, then listen and shadow), not the other way round. Do not end the summary screen with a passive list of today's items. Replace it with a 3–5-item quick recall, or drop it and put the time into the next day's relearning.

Sources:

- Adesope, O. O., Trevisan, D. A., & Sundararajan, N. (2017). Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing. Review of Educational Research, 87(3), 659–701. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306

### Effortful recall and production formats (short answer, typed or spoken or written recall) produce more durable learning than recognition formats like multiple choice. Practice also transfers best when its format matches how the knowledge will be used.

**Evidence:** strong

Rowland (2014): initial recall tests gave larger testing benefits than recognition tests. Kang, McDermott & Roediger (2007): with corrective feedback, short-answer quizzes produced the best 3-day retention. Without feedback, multiple-choice quizzes were no better than rereading. Webb (2009), Japanese EFL learners studying word pairs: learning direction changed what was learned. Productive (L1→L2) learning gave larger gains in more aspects of word knowledge (productive meaning, syntax, grammatical functions). Receptive learning gave larger gains in receptive meaning. Webb concluded that if only one direction is used, productive may be more effective. Yang et al. (2021) list format consistency and material matching as moderators of the classroom testing effect. Boundary: Webb is a single study, and it tested English word pairs, not kanji.

**For Issho:** Give each kanji at least two separately scheduled FSRS cards. (a) Receptive: read the kanji inside a known word (reading plus meaning). (b) Productive: write it from memory from its meaning and reading. Use multiple choice only as scaffolding on the first day, never in reviews. For the survival words both partners need to say in chat and role-play, add English→Japanese spoken or typed production cards. Words needed only for reading can stay receptive. That keeps the card count, and so the workload, down.

Sources:

- Rowland, C. A. (2014). Psychological Bulletin, 140(6), 1432–1463. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559
- Kang, S. H. K., McDermott, K. B., & Roediger, H. L. (2007). Test format and corrective feedback modify the effect of testing on long-term retention. European Journal of Cognitive Psychology, 19(4–5), 528–558. https://doi.org/10.1080/09541440601056620
- Webb, S. (2009). The effects of receptive and productive learning of word pairs on vocabulary knowledge. RELC Journal, 40(3), 360–376. https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854

### Items should keep being retrieved after the first correct recall. Dropping items once they feel known, whether the learner chooses to or the schedule does it, reduces long-term retention.

**Evidence:** moderate

Karpicke & Roediger (2008), 40 students learning 40 Swahili–English pairs: by the end of learning, every group was near 100%. One week later, the groups that kept being tested on all pairs recalled about 80%. Groups that stopped testing recalled items recalled about 35%. Samples were small (10 per group). Soderstrom, Kerr & Bjork (2016) replicated the result but showed part of it was a spacing confound. When spacing was equated within subjects, restudy also helped, but testing still helped. Kornell & Bjork (2008): letting learners drop flashcards they judged learned caused small but consistent learning losses across four experiments.

**For Issho:** Don't offer an 'I know this, retire it' button in normal flow. 'Easy' should lengthen the interval, never remove the card. If there is a skip for items a learner truly already knows, require a production test (write it from memory) to pass it, and still schedule it at a long interval.

Sources:

- Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. Science, 319(5865), 966–968. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/
- APA Monitor coverage (80% vs ~35%): https://www.apa.org/monitor/2008/06/testing
- Soderstrom, N. C., Kerr, T. K., & Bjork, R. A. (2016). The critical importance of retrieval—and spacing—for learning. Psychological Science, 27(2), 223–230. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615617778
- Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008). Optimising self-regulated study: The benefits—and costs—of dropping flashcards. Memory, 16(2), 125–136. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210701763899

### Spacing study sessions apart beats massing them. The best gap grows with how long the knowledge must last, but as a share of that time it shrinks: about 20–40% of a 1-week horizon, about 5–10% of a 1-year horizon. Gaps that are too short cost more than gaps that are somewhat too long.

**Evidence:** strong

Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis, 839 assessments from 317 experiments: the gap between sessions and the retention interval jointly determine retention, and the best gap rises with the retention interval. Cepeda et al. (2008), more than 1,350 people learning facts, gaps up to 3.5 months, tests up to 1 year: longer gaps first raised then slowly lowered final recall (an inverted U). The best gap was about 20–40% of a 1-week delay and about 5–10% of a 1-year delay. Boundary: trivia facts and word pairs, two study sessions only. FSRS generalizes this to many reviews per item.

**For Issho:** FSRS already spaces individual cards, but the app's own content choices should space too. (1) The i+1 reading sentences should mostly recycle words learned 1–14 days ago, not only today's items. (2) The three together sessions a week should be built from items introduced about 2–10 days earlier, plus each partner's recent lapses, not that day's new items. (3) Add a monthly 'mixed review' couple quiz drawn from the whole history, to cover long horizons.

Sources:

- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354
- Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. Psychological Science, 19(11), 1095–1102. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/ (PDF: https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)

### In L2 learning specifically, spacing has a medium-to-large effect. Longer spacing wins on delayed tests even though short spacing looks just as good right after practice. Very wide spacing keeps foreign vocabulary for years with fewer sessions.

**Evidence:** strong

Kim & Webb (2022) L2 meta-analysis, 48 experiments, 98 effect sizes, N = 3,411: spaced beat massed with a medium-to-large effect. Shorter spacing matched longer spacing on immediate posttests but did worse on delayed posttests. Equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent. Differences between studies were explained by learning target, number of sessions, practice type, activity type, feedback timing and retention interval. I could not get the full text, so no pooled g is reported here. Bahrick et al. (1993), 4 family members, 300 English–foreign word pairs over 9 years: 13 relearning sessions 56 days apart gave retention comparable to 26 sessions 14 days apart, measured up to 5 years later. That sample is tiny but the study runs unusually long.

**For Issho:** Let intervals grow to many months. Leave FSRS's maximum interval high (Anki's default is 100 years) and don't cap it at 30–90 days. Tell the couple plainly that the early slowdown from spacing pays off on delayed recall. Treat a missed day as harmless: the due queue simply grows. A 'catch-up' mode should spread the backlog over several days instead of forcing a cram.

Sources:

- Kim, S. K., & Webb, S. (2022). The effects of spaced practice on second language learning: A meta-analysis. Language Learning, 72(1), 269–319. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479
- Bahrick, H. P., Bahrick, L. E., Bahrick, A. S., & Bahrick, P. E. (1993). Maintenance of foreign language vocabulary and the spacing effect. Psychological Science, 4(5), 316–321. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x
- Anki Manual, Deck Options (maximum interval default): https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html

### What matters is the total amount of spacing between retrievals, including within one session. The exact shape of the schedule (expanding, equal or contracting) matters little. Repeating the same item back-to-back is the least effective arrangement.

**Evidence:** strong

Karpicke & Bauernschmidt (2011), Swahili–English pairs: long gaps between three tests improved long-term retention by about 200% over tests with no gap. Expanding, equal and contracting schedules did not differ. Kim & Webb (2022) also found equal = expanding. Kornell (2009), GRE-style word pairs: spacing within a session (one big 20-card stack) beat massing (four 5-card stacks) for 90% of participants. Nakata & Webb (2016): once spacing is equated, splitting items into small or large sets has little effect. Spacing is what matters.

**For Issho:** Restructure the new-item block. The current design (trace → guided → memory for the same kanji back-to-back, then first-check right after) is mostly massed. Better: teach a batch of 4–6 items with trace and guided passes, then cycle back for the from-memory write of each one after several other items have come between. Move 'first-check retrieval' after the reading block, so several minutes have passed since teaching. Mix due reviews in among new-item recalls to create natural gaps.

Sources:

- Karpicke, J. D., & Bauernschmidt, A. (2011). Spaced retrieval: Absolute spacing enhances learning regardless of relative spacing. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 37(5), 1250–1257. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023436
- Kornell, N. (2009). Optimising learning using flashcards: Spacing is more effective than cramming. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 23(9), 1297–1317. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1537
- Nakata, T., & Webb, S. (2016). Does studying vocabulary in smaller sets increase learning? Studies in Second Language Acquisition. https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-studying-vocabulary-in-smaller-sets-increase-learning/E17B75ABAE1300734AF014C363D59FBC

### A desired retention around 0.85–0.92 is the efficient zone. Workload climbs steeply above about 0.95, and scheduling items when predicted recall is very low or very high is inefficient.

**Evidence:** moderate

The Anki manual (FSRS) sets the default desired retention at 0.90 as a balance. It says workload rises quickly above 90%, can become overwhelming above 97%, and recommends staying below 97%. From the published FSRS-4.5 forgetting curve, R = (1 + (19/81)·t/S)^−0.5, I derived how intervals scale with desired retention. Relative to 0.90, intervals are about 2.4× longer at 0.80 and 1.64× at 0.85. They are about 0.46× at 0.95, 0.27× at 0.97 and 0.087× at 0.99. That means roughly 2×, 3.7× and 11.6× as many reviews per item at 0.95, 0.97 and 0.99. This is my calculation, not a study. It ignores lapses and the larger stability gains FSRS gives when reviews happen at lower recall, both of which narrow the gap. Eglington & Pavlik (2020): 291 online participants learning 30 Japanese–English pairs, 22-minute session, test 3 days later. Practicing items when predicted recall reached 0.86 or 0.94 gave about 40% more recall than a fixed-spacing schedule. Thresholds of 0.40 and 0.98 did worse (an inverted U). The reason: correct trials are fast, while failures cost feedback time. Boundary: crowdsourced sample, short horizon, paired associates only.

**For Issho:** Default desired retention 0.90 for both partners. Allow 0.85 for low-priority recognition-only vocabulary when the backlog grows. Never offer above 0.95. Show a projected 'minutes per day' figure whenever someone changes it. Handwriting production cards are costly to fail (rewriting plus feedback), which argues for keeping them at about 0.90 rather than lower.

Sources:

- Anki Manual, Deck Options – FSRS Desired Retention: https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html
- FSRS algorithm description (forgetting curve and interval formula): https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm
- FSRS wiki, The Optimal Retention (workload/knowledge trade-off, CMRR): https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-Optimal-Retention
- Eglington, L. G., & Pavlik, P. I. Jr. (2020). Optimizing practice scheduling requires quantitative tracking of individual item performance. npj Science of Learning, 5, 15. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567101

### FSRS predicts each learner's forgetting better than older heuristic and model-based schedulers. Peer-reviewed work shows recall-probability-driven scheduling beats fixed heuristics on real language-app data. But no randomized trial shows FSRS produces better learning than SM-2.

**Evidence:** practitioner

Open srs-benchmark: about 10,000 Anki users, about 727M reviews, time-based train/test splits. FSRS-6 scored log loss 0.346, RMSE(bins) 0.065, AUC 0.703. Duolingo-style half-life regression (HLR) scored 0.469, 0.128 and 0.637, and Ebisu v2 scored 0.499, 0.163 and 0.605. Neural models such as RWKV scored better still but use millions of parameters. Classic SM-2 outputs no probabilities, so the benchmark has to use an adapted variant. Secondary sources say FSRS beats it for most users, but I could not verify those percentages. Peer-reviewed: Ye, Su & Cao (KDD 2022) used 220M MaiMemo vocabulary logs to build an optimal-cost scheduler, reported a 12.6% improvement over prior methods, and deployed it. Tabibian et al. (2019, PNAS) used Duolingo data: learners whose review timing matched a recall-probability-driven schedule memorized better than learners on heuristic schedules. That was a natural experiment, not an RCT. Note that these results measure prediction accuracy or observational outcomes.

**For Issho:** Keep FSRS. Fit separate parameters for each partner. Re-optimize periodically once each has a substantial review history; there is no need to optimize from day one. Consider separate presets for handwriting-production cards and recognition cards, since their difficulty and time costs differ. Following the Anki manual, keep learning and relearning steps few and under one day (e.g., one 10-minute step), and let FSRS set the first next-day interval.

Sources:

- open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark: https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark
- Ye, J., Su, J., & Cao, Y. (2022). A stochastic shortest path algorithm for optimizing spaced repetition scheduling. Proceedings of the 28th ACM SIGKDD Conference, 4381–4390. https://scholar.hit.edu.cn/en/publications/a-stochastic-shortest-path-algorithm-for-optimizing-spaced-repeti/ (dblp: https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/kdd/YeSC22.html)
- Tabibian, B., Upadhyay, U., De, A., Zarezade, A., Schölkopf, B., & Gomez-Rodriguez, M. (2019). Enhancing human learning via spaced repetition optimization. PNAS, 116(10), 3988–3993. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1815156116
- Anki Manual, Deck Options (learning steps with FSRS): https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html

### Successive relearning works: practice each item to a correct recall at first, then bring it back for correct recall in several widely spaced sessions. It produces durable knowledge for few extra trials, and spaced correct recalls are worth far more than the same number of correct recalls in one session.

**Evidence:** strong

Rawson & Dunlosky (2011), 3 experiments, 533 students, more than 100k responses, tests 1–4 months later: the initial criterion (1–4 correct recalls) and the number of relearning sessions (1–5) were subadditive. Relearning greatly improved durability at small cost. Their recommendation: 3 correct recalls initially, then 3 widely spaced relearning sessions. Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli (2013): in real psychology courses, successive relearning raised exam performance by about a letter grade over spaced restudy and baseline. Rawson et al. (2018), per the APS summary: one correct recall in each of three spaced sessions gave more than twice the recall of three correct recalls in a single session. A secondary source gives 68% vs 26%. Vaughn & Rawson (2011), Lithuanian–English pairs: higher criterion levels strengthened associative, target and cue memory. Boundary: most of this work is with undergraduates and concept or definition material, though word-pair data agree.

**For Issho:** For new items, aim for 1–3 correct recalls in the first session, spread within the session (see the spacing finding above). Then make sure there are about 3 successful spaced recalls on later days (FSRS will roughly give day 1, a few days, about 1–2 weeks). Only count an item as 'learned' in progress stats after 3 spaced successes, so the dashboard doesn't reward cramming. When a review fails, relearn it to one correct recall later in the same session, after other items have come between, not instantly.

Sources:

- Rawson, K. A., & Dunlosky, J. (2011). Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough? Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 140(3), 283–302. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956
- Rawson, K. A., Dunlosky, J., & Sciartelli, S. M. (2013). The power of successive relearning. Educational Psychology Review, 25(4), 523–548. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4 (summary: http://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/2018/successive-relearning)
- Rawson, K. A., Vaughn, K. E., Walsh, M., & Dunlosky, J. (2018). Investigating and explaining the effects of successive relearning on long-term retention. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 24(1), 57–71. https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000146 (APS Observer summary: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology)
- Rawson, K. A., & Dunlosky, J. (2022). Successive relearning: An underexplored but potent technique for obtaining and maintaining knowledge. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 31(4), 362–368. https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214221100484
- Vaughn, K. E., & Rawson, K. A. (2011). Diagnosing criterion-level effects on memory. Psychological Science, 22(9), 1127–1131. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417724

### Extra repetitions within one session give diminishing returns, and overlearning fades within weeks. Time is better spent on spaced relearning than on drilling the same item repeatedly in one sitting.

**Evidence:** moderate

Nakata (2017), 98 Japanese learners, 16 English–Japanese pairs, tests more than 2 weeks later: 5 or 7 retrievals in one session beat 1 or 3 in absolute terms. But per unit of time on task, a single retrieval gave the largest gain. Rohrer et al. (2005), 218 students learning geography facts and word definitions: overlearning gave a clear advantage at 1 week that had almost disappeared by about 4 weeks, which the authors called inefficient. Driskell, Willis & Copper (1992) meta-analysis: overlearning has a moderate effect that depends on the amount of overlearning and the task, and shrinks as the retention interval grows. The Anki manual also says repeating a card several times in one day contributes little to long-term memory.

**For Issho:** Cap repetitions for each new item in a session at about 3 retrievals (the memory write, first-check, and one appearance in reading). No 'write it 10 times' drills. Each partner then saves several minutes a day for more new items or for the next morning's relearning. The one reasonable exception is kana in the first 1–2 weeks: a small closed set that everything else depends on can justify a bit more same-day practice. That is my inference, not tested.

Sources:

### Whether interleaving helps depends on the material. It helps when learners must tell similar categories apart (visual categories, grammar forms practiced across sessions). For learning word-pairs, blocking did better, and introducing similar items together causes interference errors.

**Evidence:** moderate

Brunmair & Richter (2019) meta-analysis, 59 studies, 238 effect sizes: overall g = 0.42. Paintings and visual material g = 0.67, math g = 0.34, word material g = −0.39 (blocking better). Interleaving helped more when categories were similar to each other but varied inside, and when material was complex. Nakata & Suzuki (2019, SSLA), 133 learners, 48 pairs: semantically related words were not recalled worse overall, but produced more interference errors, and spacing helped unrelated words more. Nakata & Suzuki (2019, MLJ), 115 Japanese EFL learners, 5 grammar structures: interleaving caused more errors during practice but beat blocking on a 1-week test, especially for lower-scoring learners. Pan et al. (2019), Spanish preterite vs imperfect: no interleaving benefit in single-session training, a substantial benefit when interleaving ran across multiple sessions (1-week test). Boundary: no kanji-specific interleaving study found.

**For Issho:** (1) When choosing new items, don't introduce look-alike kanji (e.g., 未/末, 土/士) or tight semantic sets on the same day; stagger them by days. Large sets like all the numbers or days of the week could be split across a few days. (2) Once both members of a confusable pair are known, add discrimination cards that mix them ('which is まつ = end?'). (3) Grammar: introduce related points on different days (は vs が, て-form groups), then mix them in later practice and in the role-plays. Mixing pays off across sessions, not within one cram.

Sources:

- Nakata, T., & Suzuki, Y. (2019). Mixing grammar exercises facilitates long-term retention: Effects of blocking, interleaving, and increasing practice. The Modern Language Journal, 103(3), 629–647. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12581
- Pan, S. C., Tajran, J., Lovelett, J. T., Osuna, J., & Rickard, T. C. (2019). Does interleaved practice enhance foreign language learning? The effects of training schedule on Spanish verb conjugation skills. Journal of Educational Psychology, 111(7), 1172–1188. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000336

### Showing the correct answer after an error is what makes feedback work. Feedback after correct answers adds little. Whether feedback comes immediately or later matters much less, and evidence on timing is mixed.

**Evidence:** strong

Pashler et al. (2005), 258 participants, Luganda–English pairs: showing the correct answer after an error raised 1-week retention by 494%. Feedback after correct responses made little difference, even for low-confidence ones. A later study, Fazio et al. (2010, reported secondhand), found plain right/wrong feedback didn't correct errors. Kulik & Kulik (1988), 53 studies: in applied classroom studies immediate feedback usually beat delayed, while lab studies of test content often showed the reverse. Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn (2009): delayed feedback beat immediate for vocabulary. Nakata (2014/2015), 98 Japanese students, 16 pairs: once lag to test was controlled, feedback timing had little effect on L2 vocabulary.

**For Issho:** After any miss, always show the full answer: the kanji with an animated stroke order, its reading with audio, and the word in a known sentence. A red X alone is not enough. After a fast correct answer, keep the reveal minimal to save seconds. On the from-memory handwriting pass, let the learner finish the whole character before marking strokes, so the recall attempt isn't interrupted, then show the corrections. That is inferred from the 'feedback after errors' evidence; no kanji-specific test. In the partners' quiz-each-other mode, reveal the answer right after each attempt.

Sources:

- Pashler, H., Cepeda, N. J., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2005). When does feedback facilitate learning of words? Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 31(1), 3–8. https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/PCWR2005.pdf
- Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1988). Timing of feedback and verbal learning. Review of Educational Research, 58(1), 79–97. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001079

### Errors made with high confidence are the ones most likely to be corrected after feedback (the hypercorrection effect), so confident mistakes are a good moment to teach.

**Evidence:** moderate

Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001): contrary to the prediction that strongly held errors resist correction, errors made with high confidence were the most likely to be fixed on a retest after feedback. Secondary reports of later work (Fazio et al. 2010; Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2008) say feedback also helps retention of correct answers given with low confidence. Boundary: general-knowledge questions. I found no L2-specific confirmation in this pass.

**For Issho:** Track confidence from existing signals: the FSRS grade (Easy/Good/Hard) and response time. When a card the learner last rated 'Easy', or answered instantly, now fails, show richer feedback (component breakdown, mnemonic, two example words) and flag it 'surprise miss'. When a correct answer was rated 'Hard', still show the answer briefly. Don't add a separate confidence slider, which would cost time on every card.

Sources:

- Butterfield, B., & Metcalfe, J. (2001). Errors committed with high confidence are hypercorrected. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 27(6), 1491–1494. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491
- Secondary note on Fazio et al. (2010) and Butler et al. (2008) via Pashler et al. citing literature: https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/PCWR2005.pdf

### Generating an answer, or guessing before being shown, improves memory compared with just reading. The pre-guess benefit is reliable when the guess can relate in meaning to the answer, and weak or absent for arbitrary pairings.

**Evidence:** moderate

Bertsch et al. (2007) meta-analysis, 86 studies, 445 effect sizes: generating beat reading by an average d ≈ 0.40, with considerable variation between moderators. Potts & Shanks (2014), 4 experiments: generating mostly wrong answers and then getting feedback beat reading or choosing, for unfamiliar English definitions and for foreign-vocabulary translations. Participants didn't realize it helped. Huelser & Metcalfe (2012) and Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012): the pretest or error benefit appeared only when cue and target were related in meaning. Unrelated pairs showed no benefit, and the benefit vanished if a delay separated the guess from the feedback. Pan & Carpenter (2023) review: pretesting often helps, but the size depends on procedure and on how learning is measured.

**For Issho:** Before revealing a kanji whose meaning can be reasoned from known parts (休 = person + tree, 明 = sun + moon), add a 5-second ungraded 'guess the meaning' step, with the feedback shown right away. Skip pre-guesses for arbitrary mappings (kana sounds, most on'yomi), where they cost time for no clear benefit. For grammar, have learners try to say the target sentence before the explanation. Keep pre-guesses ungraded and out of FSRS.

Sources:

- Bertsch, S., Pesta, B. J., Wiscott, R., & McDaniel, M. A. (2007). The generation effect: A meta-analytic review. Memory & Cognition, 35(2), 201–210. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441
- Potts, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2014). The benefit of generating errors during learning. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143(2), 644–667. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033194
- Huelser, B. J., & Metcalfe, J. (2012). Making related errors facilitates learning, but learners do not know it. Memory & Cognition, 40(4), 514–527. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-011-0167-z
- Grimaldi, P. J., & Karpicke, J. D. (2012). When and why do retrieval attempts enhance subsequent encoding? Memory & Cognition, 40(4), 505–513. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-011-0174-0
- Pan, S. C., & Carpenter, S. K. (2023). Prequestioning and pretesting effects: A review of empirical research, theoretical perspectives, and implications for educational practice. Educational Psychology Review, 35(4). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5

### Sleep soon after learning protects new vocabulary. Learning in the evening, sleeping, then relearning the next morning halves the relearning effort and improves retention months later. Sleep also consolidates motor-sequence skill, which plausibly includes stroke sequences (untested).

**Evidence:** moderate

Gais, Lucas & Born (2006), high-school students learning vocabulary: recall was better when sleep came within a few hours of learning, whatever the time of day. Sleep deprivation hurt recall even after a recovery night. Mazza et al. (2016), 40 adults, 16 Swahili–French pairs, two sessions 12 hours apart: the evening-sleep-morning group needed about 3 trials to relearn versus about 6 for the morning-evening group. At 1 week they recalled about 15 of 16 versus about 11 of 16, and the advantage was still visible at 6 months (APS release). Walker et al. (2002): a night of sleep raised speed on a motor-sequence task by about 20% with no loss of accuracy, while an equal period awake gave no significant gain. Boundary: small samples and lab word pairs. Transfer to kanji handwriting is an inference. I found no controlled evidence for an optimal clock time or session length apart from sleep proximity.

**For Issho:** Recommend doing the new-item and handwriting block in the evening, within a few hours of bed, ideally together. Add an optional 2–5-minute morning relearn of the previous night's new items, which FSRS's next-day interval already roughly gives; frame it as a morning warm-up. Splitting the daily 20–30 minutes into a short morning review sitting and an evening new-items sitting fits both the spacing and sleep evidence.

Sources:

- Gais, S., Lucas, B., & Born, J. (2006). Sleep after learning aids memory recall. Learning & Memory, 13(3), 259–262. https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.132106
- Mazza, S., Gerbier, E., Gustin, M.-P., Kasikci, Z., Koenig, O., Toppino, T. C., & Magnin, M. (2016). Relearn faster and retain longer: Along with practice, sleep makes perfect. Psychological Science, 27(10), 1321–1330. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616659930 (APS release: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/sleep-makes-relearning-faster-and-longer-lasting.html)
- Walker, M. P., Brakefield, T., Morgan, A., Hobson, J. A., & Stickgold, R. (2002). Practice with sleep makes perfect: Sleep-dependent motor skill learning. Neuron, 35(1), 205–211. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0896-6273(02)00746-8

### Learners consistently misjudge which practice works. Massed, blocked, error-free study feels better than the spaced, interleaved, error-generating study that actually works. So the app, not the learner, should control the schedule.

**Evidence:** strong

Kornell (2009): spacing beat massing for 90% of participants, yet after the first session 72% believed massing had worked better. Potts & Shanks (2014) and Huelser & Metcalfe (2012): learners didn't see the benefit of generating errors, even right after it had helped them. Kornell & Bjork (2008): self-chosen dropping of cards cost learning. Nakata & Suzuki (2019, MLJ): interleaving produced more practice errors but better retention a week later. The same metacognitive illusion shows up across these studies.

**For Issho:** Make the science-backed order the only default and don't offer 'cram' or 'study by list' modes. Add one-line explanations at the hard moments ('Spaced recalls feel harder — that's why they stick'). Make the benefit visible: show each partner their actual retention at 1 week and 1 month, taken from FSRS review logs, and how many items they now know long-term, rather than streak counts alone.

Sources:

- Kornell, N. (2009). Applied Cognitive Psychology, 23(9), 1297–1317. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1537
- Potts, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2014). Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143(2), 644–667. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033194
- Huelser, B. J., & Metcalfe, J. (2012). Memory & Cognition, 40(4), 514–527. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-011-0167-z
- Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008). Memory, 16(2), 125–136. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210701763899
- Nakata, T., & Suzuki, Y. (2019). The Modern Language Journal, 103(3), 629–647. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12581

### Teaching a partner helps the teacher mainly because teaching makes them retrieve. Teaching without notes beat teaching with notes.

**Evidence:** weak

Koh, Lee & Lim (2018): people who taught without notes, and so had to retrieve the material, beat those who taught with notes and the non-teaching controls on a 1-week comprehension test. The authors conclude the benefit of learning by teaching is largely a retrieval benefit. Boundary: a single study with prose content, not L2 vocabulary. I found no controlled studies of couples or peers quizzing each other on L2 vocabulary in this pass.

**For Issho:** Design the together features as two-way retrieval. In quiz-each-other mode, the asker's screen shows the answer, and the answerer must say or write it before the reveal. Add a 'teach me this kanji' turn where one partner explains components and a mnemonic with notes hidden, then the app shows the reference so both can check. Feed quizzes from items 2–10 days old and from each partner's recent lapses (see the spacing finding), so together time adds spaced retrieval instead of repeating today's lesson.

Sources:

- Koh, A. W. L., Lee, S. C., & Lim, S. W. H. (2018). The learning benefits of teaching: A retrieval practice hypothesis. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 32(3), 401–410. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3410

### A practice success rate of roughly 85–90% looks like a sensible difficulty target. This converges from theory, from one scheduling experiment, and from FSRS defaults, but direct evidence for human verbal learning is limited.

**Evidence:** weak

Wilson et al. (2019) showed mathematically that, for a broad class of gradient-descent learners on binary classification, the best training error rate is about 15.87% (about 85% accuracy). This was tested in artificial and biologically plausible neural networks, not in human vocabulary learning. It agrees with Eglington & Pavlik's best thresholds of 0.86–0.94 for Japanese–English pairs and with FSRS's 0.90 default. The convergence makes it reasonable to use, but it is not a direct test.

**For Issho:** Use each partner's rolling review success rate as an automatic brake on new items. If it falls below about 80% over 3 days, pause or halve new items until it recovers. If it stays above about 95% for a week with reviews under budget, offer slightly more new items. These thresholds are my heuristics, to tune from the logs.

Sources:

- Wilson, R. C., Shenhav, A., Straccia, M. A., & Cohen, J. D. (2019). The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning. Nature Communications, 10, 4646. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4
- Eglington & Pavlik (2020). npj Science of Learning, 5, 15. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567101

### There is no controlled evidence on the best number of new items per day for adults. Practitioner guidance says steady-state daily reviews run about 10× the number of new cards per day, and overloading early leads to backlog and burnout.

**Evidence:** practitioner

The Anki manual says 20 new cards a day leads to about 200 reviews a day, and warns that new users who add hundreds of cards early get overwhelmed. I found no RCT on new items per day or session length for adult self-study. My planning estimate, not a study: at about 10× reviews, 8 new cards a day gives about 80 reviews a day. Assume about 8 s per recognition review and about 20 s per handwriting review. With half of each, that is about 19 minutes of reviews, plus about 8–12 minutes teaching 8 new items through the 3-pass handwriting flow, about 27–31 minutes in total. That is the top of the stated budget. Note that one kanji plus 2–3 words can be 4–7 cards.

**For Issho:** Budget new material in cards, not 'items'. Start at about 5–8 new cards a day each (more during the kana weeks, since kana cards are quick), and adjust automatically. Pause new items when projected review time exceeds about 15–18 minutes, using the FSRS due forecast. Introduce a kanji's 1–3 words over the following days instead of all at once; this also spreads the cards and reduces interference. Show a 'tomorrow's load' estimate before the learner accepts extra new items.

Sources:

- Anki Manual, Deck Options – New cards/day: https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html
- FSRS wiki, The Optimal Retention (workload simulation): https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-Optimal-Retention

**Useful numbers**

- Testing effect, overall: g ≈ 0.50 (Rowland 2014; secondary summary); classroom g = 0.499 across 222 studies and 48,478 students (Yang et al. 2021)
- Repeated testing vs dropping recalled items: about 80% vs about 35% recall at 1 week (Karpicke & Roediger 2008, n = 40). Part of the gap is a spacing confound (Soderstrom et al. 2016)
- Long vs no gaps between 3 retrievals: about 200% better long-term retention; expanding = equal = contracting (Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011)
- Best gap between sessions: about 20–40% of the retention horizon at 1 week, about 5–10% at 1 year (Cepeda et al. 2008); 839 assessments from 317 experiments (Cepeda et al. 2006)
- L2 spacing meta-analysis: 48 experiments, 98 effect sizes, N = 3,411, medium-to-large effect; equal = expanding (Kim & Webb 2022)
- 13 sessions 56 days apart ≈ 26 sessions 14 days apart for retention up to 5 years later, 300 word pairs (Bahrick et al. 1993)
- Interleaving: overall g = 0.42; visual material/paintings g = 0.67; math g = 0.34; word material g = −0.39, blocking better (Brunmair & Richter 2019)
- Correct-answer feedback after errors: +494% 1-week retention; feedback after correct answers adds little (Pashler et al. 2005)
- Successive relearning: 3 correct recalls initially, then 3 widely spaced relearning sessions (Rawson & Dunlosky 2011, N = 533, tests 1–4 months later); about one letter grade gain in real courses (Rawson et al. 2013)
- One correct recall in each of 3 spaced sessions gave more than twice the recall of 3 correct recalls in one session (Rawson et al. 2018 via APS summary; secondary source gives 68% vs 26%)
- Within-session retrievals: 5–7 beat 1–3 in absolute terms, but 1 retrieval is most efficient per minute (Nakata 2017, n = 98, 16 pairs)
- Overlearning advantage clear at 1 week, almost undetectable by about 4 weeks (Rohrer et al. 2005)
- Spacing beat massing for 90% of learners, yet 72% believed massing was better (Kornell 2009)
- Generation effect: d ≈ 0.40, 86 studies, 445 effect sizes (Bertsch et al. 2007)
- Sleep between sessions: about 3 vs about 6 trials to relearn; about 15/16 vs 11/16 recalled at 1 week; benefit visible at 6 months (Mazza et al. 2016, n = 40)
- Sleep and motor skill: about +20% motor-sequence speed after a night of sleep, no gain after equal waking time (Walker et al. 2002)
- Efficient practice threshold: practicing at predicted recall 0.86–0.94 gave about 40% more 3-day recall than fixed spacing; 0.40 and 0.98 worse (Eglington & Pavlik 2020, 30 Japanese–English pairs, 22-minute session)
- FSRS desired retention: default 0.90; keep below 0.97 (Anki Manual)
- Interval multipliers relative to desired retention 0.90, derived from the FSRS-4.5 curve (my calculation, not a study): 0.80 → 2.4×; 0.85 → 1.64×; 0.95 → 0.46×; 0.97 → 0.27×; 0.99 → 0.087× (about 2×, 3.7× and 11.6× the reviews per item at 0.95, 0.97 and 0.99)
- srs-benchmark (about 10k users, about 727M reviews): FSRS-6 log loss 0.346 / RMSE(bins) 0.065 / AUC 0.703 vs HLR 0.469 / 0.128 / 0.637
- Workload rule of thumb: 20 new cards a day → about 200 reviews a day (Anki Manual; practitioner)
- Planning estimate (derived, not a study): 5–8 new cards a day per person fits a 20–30-minute daily budget with handwriting reviews; pause new cards when projected review time exceeds about 15–18 minutes
- Optimal difficulty in model learners: about 85% accuracy, i.e. 15.87% error (Wilson et al. 2019; theoretical, not tested on human vocabulary)

## Kanji and Japanese literacy for alphabetic (English-L1) adult beginners. Covers handwriting vs typing vs tracing vs recognition-only, stroke order, components vs whole characters, mnemonics (keyword, RTK, WaniKani), kanji in words vs alone, kanji order, on/kun readings, recognition vs production, coverage and vocabulary thresholds, and kana timelines. Notes on method: (1) The web-search budget ran out partway through. After that, claims were checked only by fetching known URLs. (2) Several requested sources could not be retrieved beyond their bibliographic record, so they are NOT used as evidence for any finding: Shimizu & Green 2002 (MLJ 86), Mori & Shimizu 2007 (FLA 40(3)), Paxton & Svetanant 2014 (IJRSLL 3(3)), Bourke 1996 (UQ PhD), and Kubota & Toyoda 2001 (ARAL 24(2)). (3) Most controlled evidence on handwriting, stroke order and radicals comes from L2 Chinese or novel-alphabet studies. Kanji-specific controlled evidence is thin and is flagged wherever it is relied on. (4) Every source marked 'via X' was read only through that secondary source.

### Handwriting practice builds memory for a character's form and its form–meaning link better than typing or just looking. It does NOT build the character–sound (reading) link; typing or sound-based practice does that better.

**Evidence:** moderate

Guan et al. (2011), adult L2 Chinese learners: an online tutor with handwriting gave better character recognition and stronger character–meaning links than reading-only, with no gain on character–phonology. Pinyin typing strengthened phonology. Lyu et al. (2021), synthetic review of 27 typing-vs-handwriting studies in Chinese learning: handwriting helps orthographic recognition and orthography–meaning mapping at character and word level, while typing helps phonology and orthography–phonology mapping. Wiley & Rapp (2021), 42 adults (36 completers) learning Arabic letters by tablet copying vs typing vs visual detection with equal exposure: all groups recognized letters equally (~95–97%). Writers were best on letter naming, letter writing (91% vs 78.5% vs 64.5%) and spelling, and they reached criterion in no more sessions (3.67 vs 3.92 vs 4.25). The only kanji-specific test is Sugaya (2025), a 5-person within-subjects pilot with non-kanji-background learners: handwriting gave better form recall, typing gave better reading recall, and meaning recall and form recognition were similar. Boundary conditions: mostly Chinese characters or a novel alphabet, short training, immediate or short-delay tests; the kanji evidence is only that pilot.

**For Issho:** Keep handwriting for every new kanji. In Wiley & Rapp it was not slower to criterion than lighter modes. But do not let a handwriting pass count as reading practice. Pair every handwriting step with an explicit sound step: hear the anchor word, then say it or type it in kana. In the FSRS model, track meaning, reading (sound) and written form as separate memory traces per kanji or word, because practice on one does not reliably transfer to the others.

- Guan, C. Q., Liu, Y., Chan, D. H. L., Ye, F., & Perfetti, C. A. (2011). Writing strengthens orthography and alphabetic-coding strengthens phonology in learning to read Chinese. Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(3), 509–522. https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/Writing%20strengthens%20Guan%20JEP.pdf (PDF link found by search; returned 404 on fetch, so details come from the search abstract)
- Lyu, B., et al. (2021). Comparison studies of typing and handwriting in Chinese language learning: A synthetic review. International Journal of Educational Research, 106, 101740. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0883035521000100

### The memory benefit of writing comes from producing the character yourself and seeing your own strokes. Tracing, or writing without visual feedback, weakens the benefit, and writing practice helps recall (production) more than recognition.

**Evidence:** moderate

Naka (1998), Japanese 1st/3rd/5th graders learning pseudo-characters and foreign letters: writing beat looking at every age. The advantage shrank when the motor action was disrupted (tracing, or writing without feedback). It did NOT shrink when stroke order was scrambled. Naka & Naoi (1995): repeated writing improved free recall but not recognition, which the authors explain as encoding specificity of visual-motor information; this appeared for both Japanese and American participants. In their Experiment 1, repeated writing did not help memory for Chinese characters, words or syllables (it helped only for graphic designs). Thomas & Dieter (1987): copying foreign-language words helped form memory codes for their written forms. Boundary conditions: the tracing-specific evidence is one study with children and pseudo-characters, and no adult kanji-L2 trial compared trace vs copy vs write-from-memory. The 'writing beats looking' part is better supported (see the previous finding).

**For Issho:** Re-weight the 3-pass flow. TRACE: one quick pass, skipped automatically when the learner already knows every component of the kanji. GUIDED: copy beside the model with the learner's own ink visible. MEMORY (the core): write from a prompt (meaning plus spoken word) with the model hidden, then reveal and compare (look–cover–write–check). Spend the saved time on a second write-from-memory in the end-of-session 'first-check retrieval', not on extra tracing.

Sources:

- Naka, M. (1998). Repeated writing facilitates children's memory for pseudocharacters and foreign letters. Memory & Cognition, 26(4), 804–809. https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/dspace/handle/2115/44681
- Naka, M., & Naoi, H. (1995). The effect of repeated writing on memory. Memory & Cognition, 23, 201–212. https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/dspace/handle/2115/44679
- Thomas, M. H., & Dieter, J. N. (1987). The positive effects of writing practice on integration of foreign words in memory. Journal of Educational Psychology, 79(3), 249–253. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.79.3.249 (abstract: https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib1980/935)

### Evidence that knowing stroke order, or watching stroke-order animations, improves character recognition or retention is weak and mixed. Animations during study can even interfere.

**Evidence:** weak

Naka (1998): scrambling stroke order did not remove the writing advantage; the motor act mattered, not the order. Hou & Jiang (2022), 40 English-L1 learners of L2 Chinese, 120 characters, within-subjects: stroke-order animations lowered immediate recognition accuracy, and there were no effects either way at one week. Via Hou & Jiang (secondary): Hsiung et al. (2017) found that learning stroke order did not improve recognition or meaning learning; Zhu & Hong (2005) found flashcard stroke animations interfered (split attention); Xu et al. (2013) found writing and animations both beat passive reading for recognition. A 2024 study reportedly found a correct-stroke-order benefit for complex characters, but it could not be verified, so it is not relied on. The practitioner reason for stroke order (legibility, balanced proportions, readability of handwriting and cursive by Japanese readers) is real but has not been tested here. Genki shows stroke order for every kanji.

**For Issho:** Show the stroke-order animation once in the trace pass, then hide it. In the handwriting checker, judge memory-pass success on correct components, stroke count and placement. Treat a stroke-order deviation as a soft coaching note, not a failure, so FSRS lapses reflect memory failures rather than a convention. Do not keep animations or colored overlays on screen during recall tests.

Sources:

- Naka, M. (1998). Memory & Cognition, 26(4), 804–809. https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/dspace/handle/2115/44681
- Hou, F., & Jiang, X. (2022). Interference effects of radical markings and stroke order animations on Chinese character learning among L2 learners. Frontiers in Psychology, 13, 783613. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.783613
- GENKI 3rd ed., 'What is GENKI?' (Japan Times) — kanji lists show stroke count and stroke order. https://genki3.japantimes.co.jp/en/intro/

### Pointing out a kanji's components (radicals and parts) when the learner first meets it improves learning for novices. Alphabetic-L1 learners otherwise tend to treat kanji as unanalyzed wholes, and component (graphemic) awareness goes with better reading. How the components are displayed matters.

**Evidence:** moderate

Taft & Chung (1999), Australian novices learning 24 characters, each built from two of 16 radicals: recall was best when radicals were introduced at the first presentation, better than learning radicals beforehand, introducing them later, or never. This was a lab study. Via Hou & Jiang (2022): Xu et al. (2014) found that radical-based grouping helped beginners' meaning recall and radical awareness, with no difference for intermediate learners. Toyoda (1998): alphabetic-background learners processed kanji as wholes and struggled to segment them into graphemes; graphemic awareness was linked to reading comprehension (correlational). Kess & Miyamoto (1999), via Rothville (2023): more than 80% of kanji contain a semantic component, while the phonetic-component cue to pronunciation is not very reliable. Mori's Georgetown project (2014–15; preliminary, uncontrolled): radical-awareness activities increased students' attention to structure and their inference of unfamiliar kanji. Counter-evidence on display: Hou & Jiang found that color-highlighted radical markings slowed and reduced immediate recognition, probably through extra cognitive load.

**For Issho:** Teach each component just in time, as a 'radical' new item in the same session, right before the first kanji that uses it. Every new-kanji card should show a small, plain decomposition (parts with names and meanings) at first exposure, not color overlays on the glyph, and none during tests. Add a cheap discrimination drill ('which part means water?') and a 'kanji that share this part' link list. Put high-productivity semantic components early (e.g., 氵 亻 扌 口 言 糸 木 日 月 心/忄).

Sources:

- Taft, M., & Chung, K. (1999). Using radicals in teaching Chinese characters to second language learners. Psychologia, 42(4), 243–251. https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/using-radicals-in-teaching-chinese-characters-to-second-language-/
- Hou, F., & Jiang, X. (2022). Frontiers in Psychology, 13, 783613. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.783613
- Toyoda, E. (1998). Teaching kanji by focusing on learners' development of graphemic awareness. Australian Review of Applied Linguistics, Series S, 15, 155–168. https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/aralss.15.10toy
- Rothville, K. (2023). Developing a Japanese Vocabulary Levels Test for the purposes of extensive reading. Journal of Extensive Reading, 11(2) (cites Kess & Miyamoto, 1999). https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/view/1221
- Mori, Y. (2014–15). Design of online kanji learning lessons… Georgetown University ITEL project (preliminary findings). https://itel.georgetown.edu/projects/mori

### Mnemonic pictures and stories help English-L1 beginners encode kana and kanji meanings, including in real courses. The gains are modest and fade without retrieval practice, and mnemonics backfire when they are overly complex or tied only to meaning and not to reading.

**Evidence:** moderate

Matsunaga (2003), 66 US university beginners, 40 hiragana, four methods (picture+sound, picture only, sound only, flashcards): picture+sound mnemonics helped the immediate self-test only for Roman-script-L1 learners, and gave no advantage for either group on recall 2–5 days later. Manalo, Mizutani & Trafford (2004), Auckland first-year course: 27 volunteers, mostly struggling students, took optional pictorial-mnemonic tutorials for hiragana, katakana, vocabulary and kanji. Their marks did not rise significantly, but the pass rate did; there was no randomized control. Rose (2013), year-long qualitative study of university kanji learners: mnemonics helped when used meaningfully. Overly complex stories caused the meaning to be lost, and meaning-only associations left some learners unable to read the kanji. Taguchi (2006), keyword method over a semester in a university Japanese class: a marginal effect on productive vocabulary (p < .10). Only half the treatment group adopted it, choosing it only when it cost less effort than their own techniques. Oxford EU project (2013–17): strategy instruction improved kanji memorization in 118 school learners, and pictorial association was the most-used strategy. RTK (Heisig) relies on learner-built stories from 'primitives' but has no controlled evaluation. WaniKani provides a mnemonic for every radical, kanji and word, also without controlled evidence; both are practitioner methods. Manalo et al. judged the often-cited Quackenbush et al. (1989) 'Hiragana in 48 minutes' study too flawed to show efficacy.

**For Issho:** Give every new kana and kanji one short, concrete mnemonic (one sentence at most) built only from component names the learner already knows, plus a 'write your own' field. For kanji, also attach a short sound hook for the anchor word's reading, not just the meaning. Show the mnemonic on the learning card and after a failed recall, never as the cue during a test. In Together, let partners see, swap and upvote each other's mnemonics; self-made ones count as meaningful elaboration.

Sources:

### Keyword-mnemonic memories form fast but are forgotten faster than meaning-based (semantic) ones unless they are reinforced by repeated testing.

**Evidence:** moderate

Wang & Thomas (1995), three experiments: the keyword method beat a semantic-context strategy on immediate cued recall, but the advantage reversed after two days, for both obscure English words and L2 vocabulary. Extra study and test opportunities helped keyword learners' long-term retention more than semantic learners'. Matsunaga (2003) showed the same immediate-only pattern for hiragana. Boundary conditions: lab word pairs under incidental-learning instructions, not kanji specifically.

**For Issho:** Mnemonic-encoded items need an early retrieval schedule: a same-session 'first-check' recall, then a review the next day (make sure FSRS's first interval for new kanji is not longer than about 1 day). Move each new kanji quickly from story to meaning in context (its anchor words inside a sentence) so the memory gains semantic links that last longer than the keyword link.

### Knowing a kanji is not the same as knowing the words it writes. Reading depends on word-level knowledge (reading plus meaning). Learners infer best when they combine kanji-part meaning with context, and kanji pay off most when they are used to link related words.

**Evidence:** moderate

Matsushita (2019; based on his 2012 PhD): knowing a kanji does not mean knowing its words. He recommends linking words through the shared kanji: learning the core meaning of 変 from 変える/変わる/変化 connects 変更, 変換, 改変 and others. On-readings have many homophones (ヘン = 変 返 辺 編 偏 片 …), so a form link helps both written and spoken vocabulary. At least 753 kanji-based elements can act as affixes. Kanji words make up ~40% of tokens in Japanese text and about two-thirds of content-word tokens (Matsushita 2014, via Rothville 2023). Mori's (1996) dissertation: 59 English-speaking college learners inferred novel kanji compounds better with both context and word parts than with either alone, with large individual differences in which cue they used. Rose (2013): kanji learned for meaning only were often unreadable.

**For Issho:** Make the word, not the bare kanji, the main SRS unit for reading. Introduce each kanji with 1–3 high-frequency anchor words that use its commonest readings, with at least one shown in a short sentence. Test readings in word or sentence context. Use bare-kanji cards only for writing and core meaning. When a new word reuses a known kanji, show a 'you know this kanji' link and the related words. Add small compound-inference items (e.g., guess 電車 from 電 + 車) once both parts are known.

### Teach on/kun readings selectively, through words, not as lists of readings per kanji. This is standard course practice, and the official Jōyō list itself documents each reading through example words.

**Evidence:** practitioner

GENKI: each kanji list gives readings, meanings, stroke order and example words, with shaded readings and words marked to memorize, so not every reading is required up front. In GENKI 2, practice sheets revisit earlier kanji to practice new readings. The official Jōyō table (2010) lists 2,136 kanji, gives each reading with example words, and indents readings that are special or narrowly used. Phonetic components are not a reliable guide to on-readings (Kess & Miyamoto via Rothville 2023). Rose (2013): learning kanji for meaning without reading leaves learners unable to read them. No controlled study comparing reading-sequencing policies was found.

**For Issho:** For each kanji, introduce only the readings used by its anchor words. Hold back other readings until a scheduled word needs them, then present that word as 'known kanji, new reading'. Never quiz isolated on/kun lists; quiz word readings by typing kana or speaking. Draw anchor-word candidates from the Jōyō example words, filtered by corpus frequency and the couple's survival topics, and tag the indented (rare) readings as 'later'.

### Coverage of running Japanese text rises steeply with frequency-ordered kanji. Words written only in kana already make up ~64% of word tokens. Adding the top 100 kanji brings words you can decode to ~74%, top 300 ~83%, top 500 ~88%, top 1,000 ~95%, top 1,500 ~98% and top 2,000 ~99%.

**Evidence:** strong

Matsushita (2019; table from his 2012 PhD, ch. 6) used the BCCWJ written corpus (33.2M word tokens, 54.9M characters), with kanji ranked by the CDJ frequency database. Hiragana-only words = 59.7% of tokens, katakana-only = 3.3%. Cumulative token coverage of words writable with kana plus the top N kanji: N=100 73.8%, 200 79.0%, 300 82.6%, 500 88.0%, 700 91.6%, 1,000 95.1%, 1,500 98.1%, 2,000 99.2%. At character level: kana = 60.2% of characters; top 500 kanji 86.0%; 1,000 94.4%; 1,500 97.8%; 2,000 99.1%. Matsushita notes that kana plus about 1,000 kanji reaches 95% coverage, which matches what roughly 6,400 lemmas give in English. Boundary conditions: these are words you can DECODE by script, not words you UNDERSTAND. Ranks shift by genre (news, fiction, manga). The numbers are descriptive corpus facts, so the evidence is strong for the numbers themselves.

**For Issho:** Order kanji mainly by corpus frequency, with component prerequisites inserted (see the kanji-order finding). Show progress as coverage milestones (100 → ~74%, 300 → ~83%, 500 → ~88%, 1,000 → ~95%). Expect diminishing returns past ~1,500 kanji; at that point move effort toward vocabulary and reading volume. Since kana-only words already cover ~60%, put furigana over every not-yet-learned kanji so real reading can start in week 1.

Sources:

- Matsushita, T. (2019). 日本語語彙習得に関わる普遍性と個別性―漢字をめぐる問題を中心に―. NINJAL 学習者コーパス・ワークショップ&シンポジウム 発表資料, pp. 48–55 (Table 1). https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/jll/lsaj/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/869b469213df7c0f6deaad728b97a6b9.pdf

### Japanese needs far more words than English for the same coverage (about 9–12k words for 95%, 20–24k for 98%). Japanese reading-comprehension research puts the known-word threshold near 96%, and near 93% with dictionary help.

**Evidence:** moderate

Matsushita (2012, p.155, via Matsushita 2019): 95% coverage takes 9,000–12,000 lemma-like words and 98% takes 20,000–24,000. English needs about 4,000 word families (≈6,400 lemmas) for 95%. Komori, Mikuni & Kondo (2004): 61 learners from China, Taiwan and Korea; known-word rate correlated r = .70 with comprehension, about 96% known words were needed to score ≥80% on comprehension tasks, and vocabulary explained ~47% of reading variance. A follow-up put the listening threshold near 93% (Mikuni et al. 2005, read only via a secondary summary). Matsushita (2014, via Rothville 2023): 93% known words for adequate comprehension with some dictionary help. Jeon & Yamashita's 2014 meta-analysis (via Matsushita 2019): vocabulary–reading r ≈ .79. Boundary conditions: the Komori sample had kanji-background L1s, so English-L1 learners may need equal or higher coverage. The English thresholds of 95% (adequate) and 98% (comfortable reading, incidental learning) come from Hu & Nation (2000) and Nation's work, as cited in Rothville (2023).

**For Issho:** The i+1 sentence generator should guarantee ≥96% known tokens, aiming for 98% when the goal is incidental learning. In practice: at most one unknown word per sentence, and roughly one per 25–50 running words in longer passages, with furigana on unknown kanji. Over the long run, vocabulary size, not kanji count, is the bottleneck. Keep high-frequency and survival words flowing alongside kanji, and use shared kanji to make each new word cheaper (see the kanji-in-words finding).

### There is no official or research-validated kanji order. Textbooks differ widely in which kanji they teach, the JLPT has published no kanji lists since 2010, and popular self-study systems order kanji by components. The most defensible approach is a hybrid: corpus frequency plus component prerequisites.

**Evidence:** practitioner

Paxton's Macquarie thesis: the kanji selected by four textbooks differed significantly from one another, although their internal orders were similar. JLPT official FAQ: lists of vocabulary, kanji and grammar ('Test Content Specifications') stopped being published with the 2010 revision, because the test targets communicative competence. Per-level kanji lists online are unofficial reconstructions. RTK teaches ~2,200 kanji (meaning and writing) ordered by how components build up, not by frequency, and defers readings to volume 2. Supporting evidence for the hybrid: frequency drives coverage (see the coverage finding), and component knowledge at first exposure aids learning (see the components finding).

**For Issho:** Generate the curriculum order like this: (1) start from a frequency rank (BCCWJ/CDJ, or a newspaper list such as Chikamatsu et al. 2000), (2) boost kanji used in the couple's survival words and role-play scenarios, (3) insert each needed component just before its first use. Avoid a pure RTK order, which delays useful kanji, and pure frequency, which ignores reusable components. Show JLPT levels only as labels, never as the ordering principle.

### University courses teach hiragana and katakana within the first few weeks and start kanji right after, not after months of kana. Kana mnemonics give English-L1 learners a quick boost, but retention still depends on spaced retrieval.

**Evidence:** practitioner

GENKI: Lesson 1 hiragana, Lesson 2 katakana, kanji from Lesson 3 at about 15 per lesson (about 9 class hours per lesson). Auckland beginners' course (Manalo et al. 2004), 4 h/week over 12 weeks: hiragana tested in weeks 1–3, katakana in the following weeks (from week 4), then vocabulary and kanji. Corpus context: katakana-only words are about 3.3% of word tokens versus 59.7% for hiragana-only (Matsushita 2019).

**For Issho:** Hiragana: about 1 week (~10 new per day) using mnemonic, write, and read-real-words steps. Katakana: week 2, lighter weighting given its share of tokens, with loanword survival words as the reading material. Start the first simple, high-frequency kanji (e.g., 一 二 三 人 日 本 大 中) by the end of week 2 while kana keeps running through FSRS as word reading. Define 'kana mastered' as fluent reading of words and short sentences, not isolated-character accuracy.

### Writing from memory (production) and recognition are partly separate skills. Writing practice mainly boosts recall of form, production is the most expensive skill per item, and popular recognition-first systems reach far larger kanji counts.

**Evidence:** moderate

Naka & Naoi (1995): repeated writing improved free recall but not recognition. Wiley & Rapp (2021): recognition was equal across groups, while writing helped the production-type tasks. Sugaya (2025): handwriting helped form recall, while form recognition was equal across modes. WaniKani, recognition and typing only with no handwriting, claims 2,000+ kanji and 6,000+ words in 'just over a year'. RTK trains writing for all ~2,200 kanji from memory but defers readings (practitioner). No controlled study was found on the best ratio of kanji-to-write versus kanji-to-read for adult L2 learners.

**For Issho:** Use a two-tier handwriting policy with a user-adjustable 'write share'. Every new kanji is handwritten at introduction and in its first few reviews to capture the encoding benefit. Long-term handwriting-recall reviews continue only for a 'write list' (e.g., the top ~300–500 by frequency plus personally useful words such as names, address and work words). The rest move to reading and meaning reviews. This protects the 20–30 min/day budget while keeping the handwriting emphasis the couple chose.

### Handwriting with a digital pen on a tablet appears to keep the neural encoding advantage over keyboard typing. Finger-writing on a phone has not been tested.

**Evidence:** weak

**For Issho:** Writing on a phone canvas is defensible, but offer a large canvas, show visible ink, support a stylus, and recommend one (or a tablet) for the memory pass. Log the input type so the couple can compare their own retention by device.

## Comprehension and input: comprehensible input (Krashen), extensive reading and tadoku, listening, known-word coverage thresholds, incidental vs intentional vocabulary learning, i+1 and sentence-card design, audio-first and reading-while-listening, dictation, shadowing, Japanese pitch accent instruction, and furigana scaffolding and fading. Note on method: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through. I could not verify controlled studies that test furigana directly, dictation as a training method, captioned video, or sentence-mining card formats, and I say so wherever it matters. Everything else was checked against abstracts or full texts (PDFs pulled and read where possible).

### Issho's current daily session is almost all deliberate study (reviews, new items, handwriting, first-check, i+1 sentences). The research-backed course design calls for roughly equal time across four strands: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. The input and fluency strands are close to missing from the solo session.

**Evidence:** practitioner

Nation's Four Strands is an expert framework that draws on the input, output, form-focused-instruction and fluency research. He gives these conditions for the meaning-focused input strand: most of the material is familiar, the learner wants to understand it, only 1–2 words per 100 (5 at most) are unknown, context can carry the meaning of new words, and there is a LARGE quantity of input. If those conditions are absent, he says the strand does not exist in the course. The fluency strand uses only familiar material, focuses on meaning, adds some push to go faster, and should take about a quarter of course time. Nation calls the equal split arbitrary but defensible. He also notes that deliberate learning is effective and works best with increasingly spaced sessions. Boundary: this is a curriculum framework, not a tested intervention.

**For Issho:** Rebalance the 20–30 min solo budget, for example: about 10–12 min for SRS reviews plus new items and handwriting (language-focused), about 6–8 min of easy reading or listening at 98% or more known coverage (meaning-focused input), and about 4–5 min of fluency work (re-listening, re-reading or shadowing already-mastered stories with a mild speed push). The output strand mostly lives in the 3×15 min together sessions (chat, role-play). Add a weekly strand-balance meter to the summary screen so the couple can see whether input and fluency time is being starved.

Sources:

- Nation, P. (2007). The four strands. Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching, 1(1), 2–13. doi:10.2167/illt039.0. https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf

### Comprehension of a text rises steadily with the percentage of known words. About 98% known running words is the usual target for unassisted reading and about 95% is a workable minimum. The 98% figure is a rule of thumb, not a sharp cliff.

**Evidence:** moderate

Hu & Nation (2000) had 66 New Zealand university learners read a narrative at 80/90/95/100% coverage. None comprehended adequately at 80%, few did at 90–95%, and the authors inferred 98% (one unknown word in 50) for most readers. Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) proposed two thresholds: 98% coverage (about 8,000 word families) as optimal and 95% (about 4,000–5,000 families) as minimal. They also found that small vocabulary gains still help comprehension even when coverage barely changes. Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011; n=661) found a roughly linear relationship with no threshold jump, and still judged 98% a reasonable target. A registered-report replication (Kremmel et al., 2023; n=104 Sri Lankan adults) could not fully replicate the original. Genre and question format changed which coverage level was 'enough'. Boundary: all of this is English-L2 research measured in word families. Japanese adds a second variable, kanji readability, so coverage should be computed for words AND for readings the learner can decode.

**For Issho:** Have the app compute each text's coverage live from each partner's own SRS state (a word counts as known when it is mature or at least reviewed). Then label texts: Easy (98% or more, for extensive reading and listening, no lookups expected), Stretch (95–98%, tap-glosses allowed), Study (under 95%, deliberate work only). Only Easy texts should count toward the 'input' minutes in finding 1. Because the curve is linear rather than a cliff, rank recommended texts by coverage instead of hard-excluding everything below 98%.

Sources:

- Hu, M., & Nation, I. S. P. (2000). Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension. Reading in a Foreign Language, 13(1), 403–430 (as described in the replication below).
- Laufer, B., & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, G. C. (2010). Lexical threshold revisited: Lexical text coverage, learners' vocabulary size and reading comprehension. Reading in a Foreign Language, 22(1), 15–30. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/206
- Schmitt, N., Jiang, X., & Grabe, W. (2011). The percentage of words known in a text and reading comprehension. The Modern Language Journal, 95(1), 26–43. doi:10.1111/j.1540-4781.2011.01146.x. https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf
- Kremmel, B., Indrarathne, B., Kormos, J., & Suzuki, S. (2023). Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension: Replicating Hu and Nation (2000). Language Learning, 73(4), 1127–1163. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622

### Listening needs somewhat lower lexical coverage than reading (about 95%). But beginners' listening ability lags well behind their reading ability, so audio needs to be pitched easier than text.

**Evidence:** moderate

Van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013) found listening comprehension was much more stable across learners above 95% coverage. A minority coped at 90%. In Brown, Waring & Donkaewbua (2008), Japanese EFL learners met 28 target words in each mode. They picked up about 15–16% from reading or reading-while-listening, but only about 2% from listening only, on meaning translation. The authors blame trouble segmenting connected speech and a coverage level set for reading rather than listening. They cite Nation's suggestion of about 1 unknown word per 100 for extensive listening. They recommend extensive listening at an easier level or a slower narration speed than learners can read. Boundary: Brown et al. is n=35, English L2. The Japanese-specific lag is an inference.

**For Issho:** Give listen-only content its own difficulty setting, one band easier than the reading level. Offer a slower narration rate for the first months, and add multiple voices as soon as possible. Do not assume that a word known in writing is known by ear. Track an 'ear-known' flag per word: recognized in a listen-only check. Use it when computing coverage for listen-only audio.

Sources:

- van Zeeland, H., & Schmitt, N. (2013). Lexical coverage in L1 and L2 listening comprehension: The same or different from reading comprehension? Applied Linguistics, 34(4), 457–479. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/ams074
- Brown, R., Waring, R., & Donkaewbua, S. (2008). Incidental vocabulary acquisition from reading, reading-while-listening, and listening to stories. Reading in a Foreign Language, 20(2), 136–163. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/174 (full text: https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/66816/1/20_2_10125_66816_brown.pdf)

### Reading while listening is the best bridge for beginners. It produces vocabulary gains equal to reading alone, learners prefer it strongly, and it showed the largest delayed effect in the incidental-learning meta-analysis. The recommended sequence is read, then read-while-listening, then listen only.

**Evidence:** moderate

In Brown et al. (2008), reading-only and reading-while-listening gains did not differ (no significant t-test difference). 72% of learners preferred reading-while-listening, 28% preferred reading, and 0% chose listening only. Learners said the narrator chunked the text for them. The authors suggest reading a book first, then reading while listening, then listening only, so the ear is primed for words already seen. Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023; 24 studies, n=2,771) found similar incidental gains for reading (17%/15%), listening (15%/13%) and reading-while-listening (13%/17%) on immediate/delayed tests. Viewing was lower (7%/5%). Reading-while-listening had the largest effect on delayed posttests, though only 2 such studies had delayed tests. Boundary: no strict 'audio-only first' (silent-period or ALG-style) method had supporting controlled evidence among the sources gathered here.

**For Issho:** Make reading-while-listening, with the current phrase highlighted, the default mode for the new 'Easy read' block. For each story, schedule a three-step ladder across days: day 1 read-while-listening, a later day listen-only (eyes off, with a one-question gist check), later still a fluency re-listen at faster speed. Do not build an audio-only-first track; the evidence favors pairing audio with text.

Sources:

- Brown, R., Waring, R., & Donkaewbua, S. (2008). Reading in a Foreign Language, 20(2), 136–163. https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/66816/1/20_2_10125_66816_brown.pdf
- Webb, S., Uchihara, T., & Yanagisawa, A. (2023). How effective is second language incidental vocabulary learning? A meta-analysis. Language Teaching, 56(2), 161–180. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507

### Incidental vocabulary pickup from input is real but small per exposure. Typically fewer than 20% of new words are retained, and words met fewer than about 8 times are rarely retained at all. Input alone cannot be the vocabulary engine for adults who want to learn fast.

**Evidence:** strong

Webb et al. (2023): mean proportions learned were 9–18% on immediate tests and 6–17% on delayed tests. The authors conclude that fewer than 20% of target words are likely retained from meaning-focused input. Waring & Takaki (2003): after a 400-headword graded reader, about 1 of 25 target items was recalled at 3 months, and none of the items met fewer than 8 times. Brown et al. (2008) similarly found about 1 of 28 meanings retained at 3 months in the reading modes, and argued the number of meetings needed is 'considerably higher' than 7–9. Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019; meta-analysis of 26 correlational studies) reported a medium relationship between repetition and incidental learning (r ≈ .34, per secondary report). Learners above basic proficiency gained more (g = 1.40 vs lower for basic), because they have spare attention for new words.

**For Issho:** Never rely on the reading block to teach new words. Every new word or kanji in i+1 sentences should enter SRS. The reading block's job is consolidation, depth (collocations, speed of recognition) and enjoyment. Because absolute beginners gain the least incidentally, keep input texts very easy early on, so words already in SRS get re-met rather than new ones being expected to stick.

Sources:

- Webb, S., Uchihara, T., & Yanagisawa, A. (2023). Language Teaching, 56(2), 161–180. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507
- Waring, R., & Takaki, M. (2003). At what rate do learners learn and retain new vocabulary from reading a graded reader? Reading in a Foreign Language, 15(2), 130–163. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/78 (PDF: https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/1626146/Waring-Takaki-2003.pdf)
- Uchihara, T., Webb, S., & Yanagisawa, A. (2019). The effects of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis of correlational studies. Language Learning, 69(3), 559–599. (listing: https://trios.tsukuba.ac.jp/en/researcher/0000004711)

### Spacing re-encounters across sessions roughly triples the delayed incidental-learning effect compared with massing them in one session. Where new words reappear in later input matters as much as how often.

**Evidence:** strong

In the Webb et al. (2023) meta-analysis, spaced conditions beat massed ones (immediate g = 1.51 vs 0.97). On delayed posttests the gap widened: g = 1.71 spaced vs 0.58 massed, a significant moderator. The authors link this to the distributed-practice literature (Kim & Webb, 2022) and say it shows the value of regular meaning-focused input over long periods. Boundary: only 8 effect sizes in the delayed spacing comparison.

**For Issho:** The content selector for i+1 sentences and Easy reads should deliberately re-surface words from roughly the last 2–21 days of new items, not words introduced earlier the same day. Set a recycling target: each new word appears in at least 8–10 input contexts (sentences, stories, role-plays) spread over the following weeks, on top of its SRS card. Log contextual encounters per word so the app can see which words are 'SRS-known but input-starved'.

Sources:

- Webb, S., Uchihara, T., & Yanagisawa, A. (2023). Language Teaching, 56(2), 161–180. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507

### Deliberate word-focused activities yield far more per word than incidental exposure: about 60% immediate and 39% delayed meaning recall. Flashcards and word lists beat fill-in-the-blank. Practicing in the productive direction (meaning to L2 form) gives larger form-recall gains.

**Evidence:** strong

Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020; 22 studies, 100 effect sizes): mean gains were 60.1% (meaning recall) and 58.5% (form recall) immediately, falling to 39.4% and 25.1% on delayed tests. Immediate meaning recall by activity: flashcards 77.0%, word lists 73.2%, writing 54.8%, fill-in-the-blanks 43.1%. Fill-in-the-blanks was significantly worse than flashcards and word lists. Productive-direction learning gave 22.4 percentage points more on form-recall tests, but this rests on only 2 studies. Minutes spent per word did not predict gains. Boundary: mostly single-session lab treatments of up to 1 day, with few delayed tests. The flashcard-specific figures rest on few studies. The intentional and incidental numbers are not strictly comparable, since intentional studies lacked control groups.

**For Issho:** Sentence-cloze cards (the i+1 sentence with a blank) should not be the main vocabulary card. Keep a word-level retrieval card with an L1 meaning, and use the sentence as context or a secondary prompt. Prioritize the production direction (English to Japanese, ending in write-from-memory) for words they need to use, which fits the existing memory-pass handwriting. Use recognition-only cards for words that are only for reading. Time per card is not the lever; retrieval and spacing are.

Sources:

- Webb, S., Yanagisawa, A., & Uchihara, T. (2020). How effective are intentional vocabulary-learning activities? A meta-analysis. The Modern Language Journal, 104(4), 715–738. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671 (author PDF: https://takumiuchihara.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/3/7/123756989/webb_yanagisawa___uchihara__2020__mlj_intentiona_vocab_learning.pdf)

### Glossing unknown words while reading raises vocabulary learning from about 27% to about 45% (immediate). L1 glosses beat L2 glosses, and multiple-choice glosses that make the reader choose a meaning work best. This is the best available indirect evidence for designing furigana and meaning lookups.

**Evidence:** strong

Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020; 42 studies, 359 effect sizes, N=3,802): glossed reading gave 45.3% immediate and 33.4% delayed retention, against 26.6% and 19.8% unglossed. Multiple-choice glosses were most effective; in-text glosses and end glossaries were least effective. L1 glosses beat L2 glosses at every proficiency level. Textual, pictorial and auditory glosses did not differ. Boundary: glosses here are meaning glosses, not pronunciation aids like furigana, so applying this to furigana is an analogy.

**For Issho:** In Stretch texts and i+1 sentences, make unknown words tap-to-reveal with an English (L1) gloss. For the 1–2 target words, use a 'guess first' multiple-choice gloss (pick the meaning from 3 options, then reveal), which turns reading into a retrieval event. Don't build an end-of-text glossary screen. A picture or audio gloss is a nice-to-have, not a priority.

Sources:

- Yanagisawa, A., Webb, S., & Uchihara, T. (2020). How do different forms of glossing contribute to L2 vocabulary learning from reading? A meta-regression analysis. Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 42(2), 411–438. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263119000688

### Extensive reading of easy, self-selected material improves reading rate and comprehension, with medium overall effects. Effects are larger for adults and when the reading is built into the curriculum. Its effect on vocabulary is small.

**Evidence:** strong

Nakanishi (2015; 34 studies, 43 effect sizes, N=3,942): d = 0.46 between groups and 0.71 pre–post. A secondary summary reports reading rate d ≈ 0.98, comprehension d ≈ 0.63 and vocabulary d ≈ 0.18. Jeon & Day (2016; 71 samples from 49 studies, N=5,919): small-to-medium effects. Adults showed larger effects than children or adolescents, web-based stories beat paper books, and extensive reading as part of the curriculum had the highest mean effect. Caveats: about half of Nakanishi's studies lacked control groups, and later authors criticize measurement quality. Almost all of these studies are of English as L2.

**For Issho:** Make the Easy-read block part of the daily session itself (it counts toward finishing the day), not an optional Library tab. That matches the 'part of curriculum' and 'digital' moderators. Measure success through reading speed (characters per minute on Easy texts) and comprehension checks, not vocabulary gains; vocabulary is SRS's job. Show reading speed as a progress metric.

Sources:

- Nakanishi, T. (2015). A meta-analysis of extensive reading research. TESOL Quarterly, 49(1), 6–37. https://metatoc.com/papers/42892-a-meta-analysis-of-extensive-reading-research
- Jeon, E.-Y., & Day, R. R. (2016). The effectiveness of ER on reading proficiency: A meta-analysis. Reading in a Foreign Language, 28(2), 246–265. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/354

### Japanese extensive reading (tadoku) is feasible for adult beginners within the first year. Beginners read large volumes of graded or children's books and their reading comprehension improves. The Japanese evidence is small-scale and largely uncontrolled.

**Evidence:** weak

Hitosugi & Day (2004): 14 second-semester University of Hawai'i learners read 443 books in 10 weeks (about 31.6 each). On the hardest reading-comprehension section they gained +0.88 points while a regular class (n=10) dropped 0.13. Their attitudes improved more than the regular class. The authors call these results indicative only (small n, not designed as research). Rothville (2025; intact cohort of 52 first-year, second-semester beginners): about half read roughly one book a week and reported a wide range of benefits; this was descriptive only. NPO Tadoku's official levels: Start = 200 words and 0–200 characters per story; L0 = 350 words, 200–400 characters; L1 = 350 words, 400–1,500 characters (about N5); L2 = 500 words, 1,500–3,000 characters (about N4); L3 = 800 words, 2,500–6,000 characters (about N3); L4 = 1,300 words; L5 = 2,000 words. Kanji are used from L0 with furigana.

**For Issho:** Start meaning-focused reading as soon as hiragana is done (week 1–2), using Start/L0-style micro-stories of 200–400 characters with pictures and audio. These can be generated in-app from each partner's known words. Target pace: about 1 'book' (micro-story set) per week in month 1, rising to several per week. Use the Tadoku level ladder (200 → 350 → 500 → 800 → 1,300 → 2,000 words) as the app's reading-level spine and unlock rule. Add a shared 'bookshelf' so the couple can see each other's finished stories.

Sources:

- Hitosugi, C. I., & Day, R. R. (2004). Extensive reading in Japanese. Reading in a Foreign Language, 16(1), 20–39. https://hdl.handle.net/10125/66593 (https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/88180585-1f26-4fa2-838d-b148217bcc00/full)
- Rothville, K. (2025). Japanese extensive reading: Responses of an intact university cohort of beginner learners. Reading in a Foreign Language, 37(1), 1–26. https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/87fe41d0-31a5-4230-b2ce-e2ed0817145d
- NPO Tadoku Supporters (n.d.). Levels explained [Japanese]. https://tadoku.org/japanese/levels/

### Tadoku reading rules (start very easy, no dictionary, skip what you don't understand, drop boring books) and furigana-first reading are expert practice. Graded readers keep furigana through the intermediate levels and remove it only gradually.

**Evidence:** practitioner

NPO Tadoku tells learners to start at the easiest level even if it feels too easy, avoid the dictionary, skip unknowns and quit books that aren't working. It suggests looking at pictures first, then listening to the audio, then listening while reading. It says to use furigana to read comfortably and only later try texts without it. Its level design keeps furigana on all kanji at the low levels. Katakana furigana disappears at Level 4, and at Level 5 furigana is removed only for kanji taught by the end of Japanese grade 2. I could not verify (within the search budget) any controlled study that tests furigana presence or fading on L2 kanji learning or reading. Furigana policy is therefore practitioner-grounded. The closest indirect evidence is the glossing meta-analysis (finding 8).

**For Issho:** Separate two furigana policies. (a) Reading and listening blocks (fluency and comprehension): show furigana on any kanji whose reading is not yet mature in that partner's SRS, so reading flow is never blocked. This fades per kanji-reading automatically and per user, so the two partners see different furigana on the same text. (b) Deliberate kanji cards and the 'first-check': no furigana. Use tap-to-reveal for kanji in the learning stage (attempt first, then reveal), never always-on. Add a weekly 'furigana-off challenge' on a re-read Easy story as a fluency task.

Sources:

- NPO Tadoku Supporters (n.d.). What is Tadoku / How to start. https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/what-is-tadoku-en/
- NPO Tadoku Supporters (n.d.). Levels explained. https://tadoku.org/japanese/levels/
- NPO Tadoku Supporters (n.d.). Our graded readers. https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/graded-readers-en

### Comprehension-based instruction works as well as production-based instruction for beginners' receptive knowledge. Production practice gives more durable productive knowledge. The modern consensus is that input-rich teaching is essential but not sufficient.

**Evidence:** moderate

Shintani, Li & Ellis (2013), a meta-analysis of comparative studies: both comprehension-based and production-based instruction had large effects. Comprehension-based was stronger on immediate receptive tests; production-based was stronger on delayed productive tests (from abstract and secondary summaries; the full text was not accessed). Lichtman & VanPatten (2021) revisit Krashen 40 years on and argue that much of his account (acquisition driven by comprehensible input, limited role of explicit rules) is broadly supported under newer names, with responses published in the same issue. The pitch-accent findings below show limits: some features are not acquired from input alone.

**For Issho:** Keep the daily input block (finding 1), and keep production in the loop too: write-from-memory, shadowing, and partner chat and role-play for durable productive knowledge. For grammar points, introduce them with several comprehension items first (pick the picture or meaning), then one or two production items, then recycle through input.

Sources:

- Shintani, N., Li, S., & Ellis, R. (2013). Comprehension-based versus production-based grammar instruction: A meta-analysis of comparative studies. Language Learning, 63(2), 296–329. (summary: https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350; author list: https://www2.kansai-u.ac.jp/shintani/publication/index.html)
- Lichtman, K., & VanPatten, B. (2021). Was Krashen right? Forty years later. Foreign Language Annals, 54(2), 283–305. https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12552

### English-speaking learners do not acquire Tokyo Japanese pitch accent from input alone. Accuracy stays near chance even after hundreds of hours of instruction or 2.5 years of study, so pitch accent needs explicit, visualized marking and dedicated practice from the start.

**Evidence:** moderate

Shport (2008): 16 intermediate (third-year) American learners with no accent instruction scored 56% correct in production and 46% in perception, no better than chance. They tended to accent every word, and the unaccented pattern and words with long vowels or moraic nasals were hardest. Muradás-Taylor (2022): 21 English-L1 learners produced 180 words in three contexts. The less experienced group averaged about 250 instruction hours and the more experienced about 970. Both groups had 43% accurate accent types, about 40% stable types, and only 18–19% accurate and stable. The author argues these learners do not store accent in long-term memory. Both note that textbooks for English speakers rarely mark accent. Muradás-Taylor also raises the alternative view that non-standard accent may be acceptable, pending comprehensibility research. Boundary: small samples and production-focused. Shport 2008 also found no production–perception correlation.

**For Issho:** Show the pitch pattern on every vocabulary card and every audio sentence from day 1, using a downstep marker or pitch line, not just H/L letters. Tag each word's accent type (heiban, atamadaka, nakadaka, odaka) in the data model. Treat heiban words and words with long vowels, ん or っ as 'high-risk' and give them extra perception items. Set realistic goals: aim for comprehensible and consistent rather than native-perfect; Shport's native speakers themselves scored 81% and 59% on her tasks.

Sources:

- Shport, I. A. (2008). Acquisition of Japanese pitch accent by American learners. In P. Heinrich & Y. Sugita (Eds.), Japanese as Foreign Language in the Age of Globalization (DIJ Monograph 43, pp. 165–187). iudicium. https://www.dijtokyo.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/165-187_ACQUISITION-OF-JAPANESE-PITCH-ACCENT-BY-AMERICAN-LEARNERS_43-Heinrich_Sugita-11.pdf
- Muradás-Taylor, B. (2022). Accuracy and stability in English speakers' production of Japanese pitch accent. Language and Speech, 65(2), 377–403. https://doi.org/10.1177/00238309211022376 (open copy: https://ray.yorksj.ac.uk/id/eprint/5391/)

### Short perceptual training with many voices and immediate feedback improves English listeners' identification of Japanese pitch-accent patterns, and the gains carry over to new words and contexts. Notation that visually mimics pitch height is the most effective visual aid.

**Evidence:** moderate

Shport (2016, SSLA) trained naive English listeners on 3 patterns in two-syllable words, with varied words, contexts and talkers. The training group got immediate feedback and improved; gains generalized to new words in new contexts. Controls improved only on the first-syllable-accented pattern. Unaccented (heiban) was hardest. Hirata, Friedman, Kaicher & Kelly (2024; Exp. 1 n=66): audio plus pitch-height notation gave gains on both trained and new words, beating flat H/L notation. A left-hand gesture interfered. In Exp. 2 (n=48) accuracy did not differ across conditions, but learners rated notation and right-hand gestures as more enjoyable and said they would practice longer. Developer studies of OJAD's 'Suzuki-kun' pitch visualizer report that visualized prosody helped more than model audio alone, mainly in Chinese learners (weak, not independent). Boundary: lab perception tasks; transfer to production is not established.

**For Issho:** Add a 2–3 minute 'Pitch ear' drill, 3–4 times a week, inside the session. Each item: hear a known word from one of several voices, choose its pattern on a pitch-line diagram, get instant feedback and replay. Mix in minimal pairs (箸/橋/端) and weight heiban items. Use pitch-height line notation everywhere, not letters. Make it a together game too: one partner says a word, the other taps the pattern, and the app adjudicates from the dictionary accent.

SLaTE paper on Suzuki-kun with Chinese learners. https://www.gavo.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/~mine/paper/PDF/2015/SLaTE_p189_t2015-9.pdf

### Shadowing (repeating speech aloud almost simultaneously) improves comprehensibility, fluency and prosody, and probably bottom-up listening for lower-proficiency learners. Evidence on individual sounds is inconclusive.

**Evidence:** moderate

A systematic review of 44 studies (Whitworth, 2024 Oxford MSc dissertation, not peer-reviewed) found shadowing improved comprehensibility, intelligibility, accentedness, fluency and prosody. Segmental effects were inconclusive, and learners rated the technique enjoyable. Hamada (2016, LTR) is cited in secondary sources as showing that lower-proficiency listeners benefit most and that gains come through better phoneme perception and word recognition. I did not read the full text; the partial-dictation pre/post tests are a secondary report. Kadota (2019) provides a theory: shadowing exercises input processing, phonological working-memory rehearsal for new words and chunks, speech production, and self-monitoring. Many primary studies are small, short and EFL-only.

**For Issho:** Keep listen and shadow in the i+1 block, but treat it as a fluency and pronunciation tool on understood material, not a comprehension tool. Lower-proficiency learners (which they are) gain most, so start early with very short, slow sentences. Add a 'record my shadow' toggle with playback against the model and a pitch-line overlay for self-monitoring.

Sources:

- Whitworth, B. N. (2024). Shadowing for pronunciation: A systematic review (MSc dissertation, University of Oxford). https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2
- Hamada, Y. (2016). Shadowing: Who benefits and how? Uncovering a booming EFL teaching technique for listening comprehension. Language Teaching Research, 20(1), 35–52. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815597504
- Kadota, S. (2019). Shadowing as a Practice in Second Language Acquisition: Connecting Inputs and Outputs. Routledge. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781351049108 (https://www.routledge.com/products/9781138485501)

### For Japanese learners, repeated shadowing of the same dialogue across sessions spread over weeks raised pitch-accent accuracy by more than 20 points. Gains from a single session did not last.

**Evidence:** weak

Rongna & Hayashi (2012): 15 Chinese and Mongolian learners of Japanese (two proficiency groups). The meaning of the dialogue was checked and explained first. Learners then shadowed it 10 times without the script, in 3 sessions (day 0, +1 week, +7 weeks), reading it aloud before and after. Accuracy on 23 nouns rose by more than 20 points and stayed around 75–86%. Speech rate also rose in both groups, and lower-proficiency learners benefited too. The authors' earlier short-term study had found accuracy up to about 90% during shadowing that fell back afterwards. No control group; non-English L1s; conference paper.

**For Issho:** Build 'shadow sets' as SRS items. Each dialogue or sentence group returns for shadowing on a spaced schedule (for example day 0, 7, 21, 49) rather than being shadowed once. Enforce the order: comprehension check, then shadow without the script (text hidden after one read-through), then a read-aloud check. Reuse the same dialogues as scripts for the couple's role-plays, so together time doubles as a spaced shadowing review.

Sources:

- Rongna, A., & Hayashi, R. (2012). Accuracy of Japanese pitch accent rises during and after shadowing training. Proceedings of Speech Prosody 2012 (6th International Conference). https://mail.sprosig.org/sp2012/uploadfiles/file/sp2012_submission_98.pdf

### Seeing the speaker's mouth improves English speakers' perception of Japanese vowel length (short vs long vowels), a mora-timing contrast; hand gestures did not help with this. I could not verify (within budget) controlled evidence for dictation as a training method.

**Evidence:** moderate

Hirata & Kelly (2010): 60 English speakers were trained audio-only, audio plus mouth, audio plus hands, or audio plus mouth plus hands. Training with lip information produced greater improvement in identifying short vs long vowels (e.g., かと vs かとう) than audio-only. The beat or sweep hand gestures did not add a benefit. A later study (JSLHR 2014) found observing or producing syllable or mora gestures had similar effects to each other. Dictation: Hamada's research reportedly used partial dictation as a measure of phoneme perception (secondary report). No controlled study of dictation as training was verified here, so its use is an extrapolation.

**For Issho:** For durational minimal pairs (long vowels, っ, ん), offer short clips of a speaker's face where storage allows (offline-first, so keep them few and small). Fall back to audio with mora-count visuals. Add an optional 30–60 second 'mora dictation' micro-task: hear a known word and type it in kana, focusing on long vowels and geminates. Label it as a perception check rather than an evidence-backed method, and keep it out of the core daily path unless their error data show durational mistakes.

Sources:

- Hirata, Y., & Kelly, S. D. (2010). Effects of lips and hands on auditory learning of second-language speech sounds. Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research, 53, 298–310. https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2009/08-0243)
- Hamada, Y. (2016). Language Teaching Research, 20(1), 35–52. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815597504

### An 'i+1' sentence with one unknown word in about 8–10 words has only 88–90% known coverage. That makes it a language-focused study item, not comprehensible input in the extensive-reading sense. Sentence-mining card formats themselves lack direct controlled evidence.

**Evidence:** practitioner

This is a derived point, combining Nation (2007): meaning-focused input requires about 1–2 unknown words per 100, with the coverage research (findings 2–3) and the activity meta-analysis (finding 7), where context-dependent fill-in-the-blank is the weakest activity. I found and verified no controlled study comparing sentence-mined cards with word cards, or the 'exactly one unknown' (i+1) constraint with other densities, within this session's search budget. The i+1 sentence format is popular community practice, not a tested method.

**For Issho:** Keep i+1 sentences as the study bridge between new items and real input, but design them for study: one target word, L1 tap-gloss, audio, pitch line, guess-first multiple choice. Do not count them toward input time. Pair every i+1 sentence set with a short Easy text where the same targets reappear in known surroundings a few days later. Have the sentence generator also produce '0-unknown' sentences (100% known) for the fluency strand, as speed-read and re-listen fodder.

Sources:

- Nation, P. (2007). The four strands. Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching, 1(1), 2–13. https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf
- Webb, S., Yanagisawa, A., & Uchihara, T. (2020). The Modern Language Journal, 104(4), 715–738. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671

**Useful numbers**

- Known-word coverage for unassisted reading: 98% (1 unknown in 50) is optimal and 95% (1 in 20) is the minimum. About 8,000 vs 4,000–5,000 word families in English. Source: Hu & Nation 2000; Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010, RFL 22(1).
- Meaning-focused input condition: only 1–2 (at most 5) unknown words per 100 running words, plus large quantities. Source: Nation 2007, Four Strands.
- Time split: about 25% of course time to each strand (meaning-focused input, output, language-focused learning, fluency). Source: Nation 2007.
- Listening coverage: at least 95% gives stable comprehension; only a minority cope at 90%. Source: van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013. Extensive listening at about 1 unknown word per 100. Source: Nation 2001, cited in Brown et al. 2008.
- Incidental vocab retained from input: 6–17% of target words on delayed tests (under 20%). By mode (immediate/delayed): reading 17/15%, listening 15/13%, reading-while-listening 13/17%, viewing 7/5%. Source: Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023.
- Spacing effect on incidental learning (delayed): g = 1.71 spaced vs 0.58 massed. Source: Webb et al. 2023.
- Encounters: words met fewer than 8 times were not retained at 3 months after a graded reader (Waring & Takaki 2003). Long-term retention likely needs 'considerably more' than 7–9 meetings (Brown et al. 2008). Repetition–learning correlation r ≈ .34 (Uchihara et al. 2019, per secondary report).
- Intentional activities: 60.1% (meaning recall) and 58.5% (form recall) immediate; 39.4% and 25.1% delayed. Immediate meaning recall: flashcards 77.0%, word lists 73.2%, writing 54.8%, fill-in-the-blank 43.1%. Productive-direction practice +22.4 points on form recall. Source: Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara 2020.
- Glossed vs unglossed reading: 45.3% vs 26.6% immediate, 33.4% vs 19.8% delayed. L1 glosses beat L2; multiple-choice glosses are best. Source: Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara 2020.
- Extensive reading overall: d = 0.46 between groups, 0.71 pre–post (Nakanishi 2015). Reading rate d ≈ 0.98, comprehension ≈ 0.63, vocabulary ≈ 0.18 (secondary summary of Nakanishi).
- Japanese ER volume for beginners: 31.6 books per student in 10 weeks, second-semester learners (Hitosugi & Day 2004). About 50% of a 52-student beginner cohort read about 1 book per week (Rothville 2025).
- NPO Tadoku level ladder (vocabulary / characters per story): Start 200 / 0–200; L0 350 / 200–400; L1 350 / 400–1,500 (≈N5); L2 500 / 1,500–3,000 (≈N4); L3 800 / 2,500–6,000 (≈N3); L4 1,300 / 5,000–15,000; L5 2,000 / 8,000–25,000. Source: tadoku.org/japanese/levels.
- Reading-while-listening narration for 400-headword readers: 93 words per minute. 72% of learners preferred reading-while-listening, 28% reading only, 0% listening only. Listening-only meaning gains were about 2% vs 15–16% for the reading modes. Source: Brown, Waring & Donkaewbua 2008.
- Pitch accent without instruction: 56% production and 46% perception, at chance, for third-year learners (Shport 2008). 43% accurate and only 18–19% accurate-and-stable for both the ~250-hour and ~970-hour learner groups (Muradás-Taylor 2022).
- Shadowing for pitch accent: 10 script-free shadowings per session × 3 sessions over 7 weeks gave more than 20 points of accuracy gain, to about 75–86% (Rongna & Hayashi 2012; no control group).

## Output, interaction and speaking: output hypothesis, interaction hypothesis, TBLT, oral and written corrective feedback, peer and partner learning (two beginners), role-play and scripted dialogue, fluency (4/3/2, four strands), speaking anxiety, and AI dialogue partners. Applied to a beginner couple who text, role-play and quiz each other in Japanese with AI tips. Method note: the web-search budget ran out near the end. Japanese-specific recast studies (Iwashita 2003; Ishida 2004) and the exact effect sizes in Mackey & Goo (2007) and Keck et al. (2006) were not verified, so no numbers from those sources are used.

### Conversational interaction helps L2 development, and it works in text chat about as well as face to face. Interaction here means negotiating meaning, getting feedback and being pushed to rephrase.

**Evidence:** strong

Long's interaction hypothesis says that negotiating meaning when communication breaks down steers the learner's attention to the forms they need. Loewen & Sato's 2018 state-of-the-art review concludes that interaction reliably promotes L2 development, but that many factors change how well it works: the interlocutor, the task, the target feature and the mode. Ziegler's 2016 meta-analysis (14 studies, 1990-2012) found interaction significantly helped development both in synchronous computer-mediated communication (live text chat, SCMC) and face to face (FTF). The difference between the two modes was small and not significant. SCMC had a small edge on written and productive outcomes. FTF had a small edge on oral and receptive outcomes. Boundary: most primary studies involve a native speaker or teacher as interlocutor, not two beginners. Mackey & Goo (2007, 26 studies) is the classic meta-analysis here, but its effect sizes could not be checked for this report.

**For Issho:** Treat the together layer as core curriculum, not a social extra. The 3x15 min/week should be scheduled tasks that force negotiation, not open chat. Info-gap tasks work well: each partner holds different information, such as two halves of a schedule or a map. In week 1, teach 'conversation-control' phrases as SRS sentence cards and make them highly fluent: もう一度お願いします, ゆっくり話してください, 〇〇って何？, どういう意味？. Count the couple's text chat as real interaction practice, since mode does not cancel the benefit.

### For novices, text chat practice can carry over to speaking and lowers anxiety more than voice chat. That makes text chat a good first rung before live speaking.

**Evidence:** moderate

Payne & Whitney (2002): learners who spent 2 of 4 weekly contact hours in a chatroom made larger oral-proficiency gains than controls. The authors explain this with Levelt's speech-production model and working memory: chat trains the same formulation processes that speaking uses. Satar & Özdener (2008): 90 novice secondary-school EFL learners, 30 each in text-chat, voice-chat and control groups. Pairs did 40-45 min chat sessions over a month, guided by 8 tasks. Speaking improved in both chat groups, but anxiety fell only in the text-chat group. A 2022 Iranian follow-up is reported to show the same pattern. Boundary: small quasi-experiments, young or university ESL/EFL learners, and English, not Japanese.

**For Issho:** Order the together features as a ladder on the same topic: (1) text chat about the topic, (2) voice messages, (3) a live spoken role-play. Japanese chat also works as reading and IME-typing retrieval practice for kana and kanji, so it serves the app's core goal. Add a daily 'question of the day' in the chat, generated from each partner's new items that day. It costs about 2 minutes and keeps output happening on days without a together session.

### Oral corrective feedback works and its effects last. Explicit feedback gives bigger immediate gains, while implicit feedback holds up better over time.

**Evidence:** strong

Li (2010) meta-analyzed 33 studies (22 published, 11 dissertations) and found an overall medium effect for corrective feedback (CF) that was maintained over time. Explicit feedback had larger immediate effects, but implicit feedback effects were more durable. Lab studies showed larger effects than classroom studies, and foreign-language settings larger than second-language settings. Lyster & Saito (2010; 15 classroom studies, N=827) also found significant, lasting CF effects. Russell & Spada (2006) reported a very large mean effect (d=1.16) of CF on L2 grammar. Boundary: most targets are single grammar features measured on tests close to the treatment, and most learners are ESL/EFL.

**For Issho:** AI tips should be a standard part of every chat and role-play. Give both kinds of feedback: a clear, explicit flag now for the short-term gain, then implicit re-encounters later for durability. Practically, each corrected error should seed an item that returns in the FSRS queue. Make it a sentence card built from the corrected version of the learner's own sentence, with a cloze on the error spot, so the correction gets spaced retrieval instead of a single exposure.

### In classroom settings, prompts beat recasts, especially on free production. Prompts push the learner to self-repair (elicitation, metalinguistic clue, clarification request, repetition). Recasts silently reformulate the learner's sentence and are often missed.

**Evidence:** strong

Lyster & Saito (2010): prompts produced larger effects than recasts, and the advantage was largest on free constructed-response measures. Lyster & Ranta (1997): recasts were the most frequent teacher move but the least likely to lead to learner repair. Elicitation produced the most repair. Brown's 2016 meta-analysis of classroom CF: recasts are 57% of teacher CF and prompts 30%. Caveats: uptake does not equal learning, and recasts can still drive development (Mackey & Philp, 1998). In L2 pronunciation, prompts improved intelligibility and recasts refined accuracy (Gooch, Saito & Lyster, 2016). Nassaji & Kartchava's 2021 handbook judges results mixed enough that no single CF type is best overall.

**For Issho:** Build AI chat tips as 'prompt, then reveal'. Step 1: highlight where the error is and its type ('particle?', 'verb form?', 'kanji choice?') and let the learner edit and resend. Step 2: if the second try is still wrong, show the corrected sentence as a diff with a one-line rule. In spoken role-plays the AI (or the partner) first says 'もう一度？' or repeats up to the error with rising intonation, then gives the model. Keep a 'just show me' toggle for tired days, since explicit feedback still works in the short term.

### Peer interaction alone builds fluency. Accuracy also improves only when peers are trained to give corrective feedback to each other.

**Evidence:** moderate

Sato & Lyster (2012) ran a quasi-experiment with 167 Japanese university EFL learners over one semester. Two groups were trained to give each other prompts or recasts, a third did peer interaction only, and a fourth was a control. Both trained-CF groups improved in accuracy and fluency (speech rate). The interaction-only group beat the control on fluency only. The authors' explanation: peer practice proceduralizes knowledge, and CF trains each learner to monitor both their own and their partner's output. Sato (2013) found that training also made learners believe more in the usefulness of peer CF. Other studies report that, untrained, peers rarely attend to form or correct each other. Boundary: intermediate learners in classes, not adult beginner spouses.

**For Issho:** Expect the couple's unassisted role-plays to build fluency but not accuracy. To get accuracy: (a) add a 2-minute onboarding called 'how to help your partner'. It teaches one signal (e.g., a hand tap or 'もう一度？') and one move (point to the problem word, don't explain the grammar). (b) Let the AI be the source of correct forms when neither partner knows. (c) After each spoken role-play, show a short AI recap with 1-2 points per person. Log partner corrections so they can feed SRS.

Sources:

### Explicit corrections from a peer can backfire. Social relationships shape whether peers attend to form at all, and some pair dynamics (dominant/passive) learn less than collaborative ones.

**Evidence:** moderate

Adams, Nuevo & Egi (2011) studied learner-to-learner interaction. Implicit peer feedback (recasts) and modified output showed only limited links to learning, and explicit corrections from peers had a negative effect. The authors suggest feedback may work differently between learners than with native speakers. Philp, Walter & Basturkmen (2010; intermediate French, role-play and discussion tasks) found that willingness to engage with language problems depended on both the task and social considerations. Peer attention also went mostly to vocabulary, not grammar. Storch (2002; 10 adult ESL pairs over a semester) identified four pair patterns: collaborative, expert/novice, dominant/dominant and dominant/passive. Collaborative and expert/novice pairs were more conducive to learning. Boundary: small samples and classroom peers, not spouses. No study of married couples learning together was found.

**For Issho:** Do not make one spouse the other's grammar police. Route explicit corrections through the AI as a neutral third party, and deliver them to each partner privately or as a shared 'we both' recap. Design for collaboration: alternate roles every round, set equal turn quotas, and frame tasks as joint goals ('get the reservation done together'). Track turn share in role-plays and chat; if one partner keeps taking most turns, the next task can hand the quieter partner the information-holder role. That nudge is a practitioner heuristic, not tested.

- Adams, R., Nuevo, A., & Egi, T. (2011). Explicit and implicit feedback, modified output, and SLA: Does explicit and implicit feedback promote learning and learner-learner interactions? - Philp, J., Walter, S., & Basturkmen, H. (2010). Peer interaction in the foreign language classroom: What factors foster a focus on form? Language Awareness, 19(4), 261-279. https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/60745/

### Two beginners 'learning each other's mistakes' is a plausible but poorly measured risk. Peers' information is often correct, but low-proficiency pairs cannot reliably settle grammar questions.

**Evidence:** weak

Long & Porter (1985) reported, as summarized in secondary sources, that learner-learner talk was not less accurate than teacher-fronted talk and gave far more speaking opportunities. Nation (1995), summarizing Newton (1995) and Joe (1995), reports that peers in communicative tasks can usually give each other useful information about vocabulary. A collaborative-writing study likewise found that the information learners gave each other was almost always correct. Against that, reviews note that limited proficiency limits how much peers can help. An editing-task study found a pair resolved questions together but still made wrong grammar decisions. No study was found that directly measures beginners absorbing a partner's errors. This finding rests on indirect evidence. Philp, Adams & Iwashita (2014) is the book-length synthesis.

**For Issho:** Mitigate rather than avoid: (1) Before any partner role-play, play a native-audio model of the same dialogue. Expert input should come first, and peer practice should mostly recycle known material. (2) Never create SRS cards from uncorrected learner sentences. Only AI- or curriculum-verified versions go in. (3) Have the AI scan a week of chat for errors both partners share and turn them into a 5-card weekly 'couple error clinic'. (4) Keep new grammar out of unassisted partner tasks until each partner has passed its first-check retrieval solo.

Sources:

- Long, M. H., & Porter, P. A. (1985). Group work, interlanguage talk, and second language acquisition. TESOL Quarterly, 19(2), 207-228. (findings via secondary sources) https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpub2/38

- Philp, J., Adams, R., & Iwashita, N. (2014). Peer interaction and second language learning. Routledge. https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9780203551349

### Producing language makes learners notice gaps. Production-based practice beats comprehension-only practice on delayed productive tests, so recognition practice alone will not build the ability to write or say Japanese.

**Evidence:** moderate

Swain proposed three functions of output: noticing gaps, testing hypotheses, and reflecting on language (metalinguistic). In Swain & Lapkin's (1995) think-aloud study, immersion students recognized a language problem 190 times while drafting and editing. That shows noticing during output, not acquisition. Critics such as Krashen and the Izumi line of work note thin evidence that output itself causes acquisition. The stronger evidence is Shintani, Li & Ellis (2013), a meta-analysis comparing comprehension-based and production-based instruction. Both had large effects. Comprehension-based was better on immediate receptive tests, and production-based was better on delayed productive tests. Boundary: mostly grammar-feature studies.

**For Issho:** Every daily session should include real production retrieval, not just recognition. Examples: English to Japanese recall, writing the kanji from memory (the app's pass-3 already does this), and building one original sentence with today's word, typed in chat or handwritten. Follow each output attempt immediately with the model, written and audio, so the learner can notice the gap. Make the 'first-check retrieval' step at least half production prompts.

Sources:

- Swain, M., & Lapkin, S. (1995). Problems in output and the cognitive processes they generate: A step towards second language learning. Applied Linguistics, 16(3), 371-391. (summarized via https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2198-output-and-beyond-dialogue-review-merrill-swains-current-approach-sla and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output)
- Shintani, N., Li, S., & Ellis, R. (2013). Comprehension-based versus production-based instruction: A meta-analysis of comparative studies. Language Learning, 63(2), 296-329. (summary: https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350)

### Task-based or task-supported teaching works, but how large the effect is remains disputed. The safest reading is 'pre-teach the items, then use them in a meaningful task'.

**Evidence:** moderate

Bryfonski & McKay (2019) meta-analyzed 52 studies of task-based programs and reported d=0.93. Xuan, Cheung & Liu (2025) re-analyzed it, citing loose inclusion criteria and oversimplified effect-size calculation, and found a more modest g=0.61 from fewer acceptable studies. Boers & Faez (2023) argue the comparative studies are not good enough yet for a meaningful meta-analysis of TBLT versus other approaches. Re-analyses still confirm benefits when programs are counted as task-supported (tasks plus explicit teaching) rather than purely task-based.

**For Issho:** Keep the app's split: solo daily sessions handle language-focused learning, and each together session is one task with a non-linguistic outcome. Examples: plan Saturday, order from a menu card, find 5 differences between two pictures, agree on a gift within a budget. Each task card needs a goal, an outcome check ('Did you agree on a time?') and target items. Avoid content-free 'just chat in Japanese' sessions for beginners.

Sources:

- Bryfonski, L., & McKay, T. H. (2019). TBLT implementation and evaluation: A meta-analysis. Language Teaching Research, 23(5), 603-632. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168817744389 ; https://todoele.net/node/239896
- Xuan, Q., Cheung, A., & Liu, J. (2025). How effective is task-based language teaching to enhance second language learning? A technical comment on Bryfonski and McKay (2019). Language Teaching Research, 29(7), 2949-2962. https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127 ; https://todoele.net/node/442136
- Boers, F., & Faez, F. (2023). Meta-analysis to estimate the relative effectiveness of TBLT programs: Are we there yet? Language Teaching Research. https://todoele.net/bibliografia/meta-analysis-estimate-relative-effectiveness-tblt-programs-are-we-there-yet

### Whatever words are written on a task's input sheet largely decide which words get used and learned during the task, and a partner who only watches the negotiation learns about as much as the one doing it.

**Evidence:** weak

Nation (1995) summarizes Newton (1995) and Joe (1995). All vocabulary negotiated in Newton's ranking and problem-solving tasks came from the written task sheet. In retelling tasks, learners produced words from the source text even when they could no longer see it, including previously unknown words. Words used in contexts different from the input were learned better. Learners who only watched negotiation learned about as much as those who negotiated. Only about 6% of negotiation concerned word meaning. Boundary: small ESL classroom studies from the 1990s.

**For Issho:** Generate role-play and task cards automatically from each partner's current 'learning/young' SRS items. Put 5-8 target words, kanji or grammar points on the card and require using them in a new context. Because observers also learn, have partners alternate between speaker and information-holder roles. When a partner hits a word they don't know, give them a pre-made card hint and show the kanji with furigana, so the moment of negotiation also exposes them to the written word.

Sources:

- Nation, P. (1995). Best practice in vocabulary teaching and learning (summarizing Newton, J. 1995, Task-based interaction and incidental vocabulary learning, Second Language Research 11(2); and Joe, A. 1995, Second Language Research 11(2)). https://cunningham.acer.edu.au/inted/eaconf95/nation2.pdf

### A balanced course gives roughly equal time to four strands: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. Fluency comes from fast, meaning-focused use of already-known material, and repeating the same speaking task under shrinking time (4/3/2) produces fluency gains that last.

**Evidence:** moderate

Nation (1995) gives the conditions for fluency work: no new language items, familiar content, focus on the message, and pressure to perform faster through repetition, time pressure or planning. He names items that need high fluency very early: numbers, polite formulas, conversation-control phrases (ask to repeat or slow down), times and quantities. He also suggests countering the boredom of repetition with a new audience or a small twist. Nation & Yamamoto (2012) set out the roughly-equal-time principle. De Jong & Perfetti (2011): 24 ESL students did 3 sessions of 4-, 3- and 2-minute speeches. Fluency rose in both groups during training, but only the same-topic repeaters kept the gains on the posttest, which the authors attribute to proceduralization. Boundary: the four-strands split is an expert framework, and 4/3/2 evidence comes from small samples of intermediate learners.

**For Issho:** The current daily design is mostly language-focused learning (FSRS, handwriting) plus some input. Add explicit fluency and output time, about 20-25% of weekly minutes. Solo: a 2-3 minute timed 'speed round' of mastered cards or easy i+1 sentences read aloud against the clock; daily number, time and price drills. Together: a beginner 4/3/2, such as retelling a prepared mini-story or describing a picture to the partner in 90, 60, then 45 seconds, same content each time. Also have each person describe their day in Japanese three times to the partner, faster each time. Use only known items.

Sources:

- Nation, P. (1995). Best practice in vocabulary teaching and learning. https://cunningham.acer.edu.au/inted/eaconf95/nation2.pdf
- Nation, I. S. P., & Yamamoto, A. (2012). Applying the four strands to language learning. International Journal of Innovation in English Language Teaching and Research, 1(2), 167-181. https://vuw-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/yamamoto-four-strands.pdf (link may be moved; secondary description: https://www.thehebrewcafe.com/main/?p=2797)
- de Jong, N., & Perfetti, C. A. (2011). Fluency training in the ESL classroom: An experimental study of fluency development and proceduralization. Language Learning, 61(2), 533-568. (summaries: https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fluency_Summer_Intern_Project_2010 ; https://hyogo-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2739/files/AA114330270140009.pdf)

### Repeating a speaking task (and planning before it) mainly raises fluency and complexity; accuracy gains are smaller and less consistent. How far apart repetitions should be spaced is still only preliminarily established.

**Evidence:** moderate

Bygate (1996, 2001) found repeated narrative and interview tasks produced more fluent and complex speech, with smaller accuracy gains. The repetition seems to work like planning, freeing attention for formulating. A 2025 systematic review of 107 oral task-repetition studies (1996-2023; Rogers and colleagues) concludes spacing has only recently been controlled and its effects are 'preliminary'. Studies that did manipulate spacing include Bui et al. 2019, Kobayashi 2022, Suzuki & Hanzawa 2023, and Rogers 2023 in Language Learning. In pre-task planning research (e.g., Foster & Skehan 1996, via secondary sources), planning strongly helped fluency and complexity, with complex effects on accuracy. A Johnson & Abdi Tabari (2022) meta-analysis on planning exists, but its numbers were not retrieved.

**For Issho:** Run each role-play 2-3 times: once, then immediately with roles swapped, then again 2-7 days later with one twist (new constraint or detail). That matches both the fluency and spacing rationale, though spacing is less proven. Give 60-90 seconds of silent planning with the task card before speaking. Because accuracy doesn't come from repetition alone, pair the second run with the AI recap from the first run.

Sources:

- Bygate, M. (2001). Effects of task repetition on the structure and control of oral language. In M. Bygate, P. Skehan, & M. Swain (Eds.), Researching pedagogic tasks. Longman. (summary: https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/events/laelpgconference/papers/v08/Bimali-Indrarathne.pdf)
- Rogers, J., et al. (2025). The time between tasks in task repetition research: A systematic review. Accepted manuscript, PolyU repository. https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/113277/1/Rogers_Time_Between_Tasks.pdf

- Johnson, M. D., & Abdi Tabari, M. (2022). Task planning and oral L2 production: A research synthesis and meta-analysis. Applied Linguistics, 43(6). https://english.ecu.edu/?p=13678

### Written corrective feedback improves accuracy in later, new writing, despite Truscott's critique. Direct correction helps most with grammar, indirect flagging helps more with word choice and may last longer, and feedback given during writing beats feedback given later.

**Evidence:** moderate

Truscott (1996) argued grammar correction is ineffective and harmful. Ferris (1999) countered that selective, systematic, accurate feedback can work. Kang & Han (2015; 21 studies) found WCF improves grammatical accuracy. Effects were moderated by proficiency, setting and genre, with no overall difference between direct vs indirect or focused vs unfocused feedback (per secondary summaries). Van Beuningen, De Jong & Kuiken (2012; N=268): both direct and indirect comprehensive CF improved accuracy in new texts 1 and 4 weeks later, compared with self-editing or extra writing practice. Direct worked best for grammatical errors and indirect for non-grammatical ones such as word choice, with indirect gains more durable. Bitchener & Knoch (2010) followed WCF effects over ten months. Shintani & Aubrey (2016) found synchronous WCF during writing produced larger accuracy gains than delayed feedback. Boundary: essays by ESL or immersion learners. Beginner Japanese chat is untested.

**For Issho:** In chat, give tips synchronously, but keep them non-blocking so the message still goes through. For grammar errors (particles, conjugation), give a direct correction plus a one-line metalinguistic note: beginners can't self-diagnose grammar. For word or kanji choice, use indirect flagging ('this word doesn't fit; try again') so the learner self-corrects. Close the loop by having the learner resend the fixed line, which turns the tip into output. Show at most 1-2 tips per message, prioritizing errors on this week's targets. That keeps the chat about meaning (practitioner heuristic, consistent with Ferris's 'selective').

Sources:

- Truscott, J. (1996). The case against grammar correction in L2 writing classes. Language Learning, 46(2), 327-369. (context: https://sfleducation.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40862-021-00110-9)
- Ferris, D. R. (1999). The case for grammar correction in L2 writing classes: A response to Truscott (1996). Journal of Second Language Writing, 8(1), 1-11. (summary: https://papers.iafor.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/ecll2014/ECLL2014_02032.pdf?t=8)
- Kang, E., & Han, Z. (2015). The efficacy of written corrective feedback in improving L2 written accuracy: A meta-analysis. The Modern Language Journal, 99(1), 1-18. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2015.12187.x ; summary https://jflr.ut.ac.ir/article_104530_en.html
- Van Beuningen, C. G., De Jong, N. H., & Kuiken, F. (2012). Evidence on the effectiveness of comprehensive error correction in second language writing. Language Learning, 62(1), 1-41. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00674.x ; OASIS summary https://hdl.handle.net/1887/75463

### L2 anxiety has a moderate negative relationship with achievement, though the causal direction is debated. Low-stakes, text-first and private practice is the best-supported way to lower it.

**Evidence:** strong

Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky (2019) meta-analyzed 97 reports (105 samples, N=19,933, 23 countries) and found a mean r = -.36 between L2 anxiety and achievement, varying by measure, level, target language and anxiety type. Later work argues anxiety may partly result from weak skills rather than only cause them. Evidence that specific interventions reduce anxiety is thinner. Satar & Özdener (2008) is one controlled example: text chat lowered anxiety in novices and voice chat did not. Boundary: the evidence is correlational. Spousal dynamics were not studied.

**For Issho:** Keep each partner's AI corrections, error counts and accuracy scores private by default. Show shared couple metrics instead: joint streaks, tasks completed, minutes spoken together. Never rank spouses against each other. Offer a solo 'rehearse with the AI' run before any live role-play. Let a speaker pass a turn once per task. End-of-session summaries should lead with communicative success ('You agreed on a dinner plan in Japanese') before error points.

Sources:

- Teimouri, Y., Goetze, J., & Plonsky, L. (2019). Second language anxiety and achievement: A meta-analysis. Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 41(2), 363-387. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000311 ; https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/second-language-anxiety-and-achievement/
- Satar, H. M., & Özdener, N. (2008). The effects of synchronous CMC on speaking proficiency and anxiety: Text versus voice chat. The Modern Language Journal, 92(4), 595-613. https://oro.open.ac.uk/17665
- Alamer, A., & Lee, J. (2021). Language achievement predicts anxiety and not the other way around. (on causal direction) https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/2021_AlamerLee_LanguageAchievement.pdf

### Practicing with AI dialogue systems or chatbots gives medium gains, including in speaking. Goal-oriented, meaning-constrained and multimodal designs do best, and lower-proficiency learners benefit.

**Evidence:** moderate

Bibauw et al. (2022; 17 studies, 100 effect sizes, multilevel model) found d=0.58 on L2 proficiency, with support for corrective feedback, gamification and form-focused or goal-oriented designs. Effects were established for lower-proficiency learners and for vocabulary, morphosyntax and accuracy. Hou & Min (2025, ReCALL; 16 studies, 89 effect sizes) found g=0.61 [0.34, 0.89] on speaking. Goal-oriented systems, which implicitly constrain meaning, had large effects and beat reactive or unconstrained ones. Mixed multimodal systems beat voice-only, which beat text-only. Corrective feedback was not a significant moderator there. A 2025 JCAL meta-analysis of generative-AI chatbots (41 studies) reported ES about 0.58. Caveats: heterogeneity is high, most studies are short, and outcome tests often match the practice format.

**For Issho:** Make AI role-plays goal-driven scenarios with a clear success condition ('buy two tickets for the 3pm train'), constrained to the learner's known plus target items. Make them multimodal: AI audio, text with furigana toggle, and spoken or typed input. Use the AI as the expert interlocutor that gives the correct model input a beginner spouse can't, and as solo rehearsal before the couple version. A good pattern is a three-way role-play: both spouses plus the AI playing the shopkeeper or waiter.

Sources:

- Bibauw, S., Van den Noortgate, W., François, T., & Desmet, P. (2022). Dialogue systems for language learning: A meta-analysis. Language Learning & Technology, 26(1), 1-24. https://hdl.handle.net/10125/73488 ; https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1035
- Hou, [first initial not retrieved], & Min, S. (2025). Dialogue-based computer-assisted language learning systems for second language speaking development: A three-level meta-analysis. ReCALL, 38(1). https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344025100268 ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B/core-reader
- Can generative AI chatbots promote second language acquisition? A meta-analysis (2025). Journal of Computer Assisted Learning. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.70060 ; https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/can-generative-scpaiscp-chatbots-promote-second-language-acquisition-a-metaanalysis/

### How accurate LLM-generated corrective feedback is for East Asian languages has barely been studied, so 'AI tips' should be treated as fallible.

**Evidence:** weak

A 2025 Computer Assisted Language Learning study had three experienced teachers rate ChatGPT-3.5 and 4.0 corrections of 153 erroneous L2 Chinese sentences, including for over-correction. GPT-4.0 feedback was more accurate, detailed and understandable, and model version mattered more than prompt language. Other small studies (e.g., a 14-person pre/post study) report AI feedback roughly matching teacher feedback for form, but weaker on content organization. No study on Japanese learner chat was found.

**For Issho:** Constrain the AI's tips to the app's own curriculum (known grammar points, lexicon, kanji list) and have it say when it is unsure. Add a one-tap 'this tip seems wrong' button. Before an AI correction becomes an SRS card, check the corrected form against the app's dictionary or grammar data. Prefer explaining over rewriting, so beginners don't absorb unexplained native-like rewrites (over-correction).

Sources:

- ChatGPT and L2 Chinese writing: Evaluating the impact of model version and prompt language on feedback (2025). Computer Assisted Language Learning. https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/chatgpt-and-l2-chinese-writing-evaluating-the-impact-of-model-ver/

### Pronunciation instruction works, and works better when it includes feedback and runs for longer. Corrective feedback measurably improved a hard L2 sound for Japanese learners of English.

**Evidence:** strong

Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015) meta-analyzed 86 reports. Pronunciation instruction had large effects (d=0.89 within-group, 0.80 between-group). Effects were larger with longer interventions, with feedback, and on more controlled outcome measures; controlled measures can overstate gains in spontaneous speech. Saito & Lyster (2012) showed that form-focused instruction plus corrective feedback improved Japanese learners' English /ɹ/. In Gooch, Saito & Lyster (2016), prompts improved intelligibility and recasts improved accuracy.

**For Issho:** The 'listen/shadow' step should include feedback, not only imitation. Record, then immediately play the learner and the model back to back, and add an ASR intelligibility check where available. A pitch-contour overlay suits Japanese pitch accent and mora timing; that specific feature is a practitioner extension. In role-plays, the AI can recast mispronounced target words. Keep pronunciation work short and frequent over months, since longer interventions show larger effects.

Sources:

- Lee, J., Jang, J., & Plonsky, L. (2015). The effectiveness of second language pronunciation instruction: A meta-analysis. Applied Linguistics, 36(3), 345-366. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amu040 ; https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-pronunciation-instruction-a-/
- Saito, K., & Lyster, R. (2012). Effects of form-focused instruction and corrective feedback on L2 pronunciation development of /ɹ/ by Japanese learners of English. Language Learning, 62(2), 595-633. https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/db78th01f
- Gooch, R., Saito, K., & Lyster, R. (2016). Effects of recasts and prompts on L2 pronunciation development. System, 60. https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10066026/1/System2017.pdf

### Explicit teaching of pragmatics (politeness, speech acts) works better than leaving learners to pick it up implicitly. Role-plays are the standard way this research measures pragmatic ability.

**Evidence:** moderate

Jeon & Kaya (2006; 13 studies) found larger effects for explicit than implicit pragmatics instruction. Later meta-analyses include Plonsky & Zhuang (2019) and Ren, Li & Lu (2023, Applied Linguistics). Their exact effect sizes were not retrieved, and one large figure (g=1.66) circulating online could not be tied to a specific meta-analysis, so it is not used. Many single studies use role-plays as the pragmatic outcome measure. Shakki (2022) reviews the meta-analyses and notes that requests, apologies and refusals are the most studied speech acts.

**For Issho:** Japanese register is a real trap for a couple: with each other they will naturally use plain or casual forms, but beginner travel and service situations need です/ます and set keigo phrases. Every role-play card should state the register and include a one-line explicit note ('Shop clerk speaks keigo; you use です/ます'). The AI should treat 'wrong register' as its own tip type. Add a few couple tasks in casual register so their home chat is pragmatically correct too.

Sources:

- Shakki, F. (2022). Meta-analysis as an emerging trend to scrutinize the effectiveness of L2 pragmatics instruction. Frontiers in Psychology, 13, 1016661. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9723127/ (summarizes Jeon & Kaya, 2006, and Plonsky & Zhuang, 2019)
- Plonsky, L., & Zhuang, J. (2019). A meta-analysis of L2 pragmatics instruction. In N. Taguchi (Ed.), The Routledge handbook of second language acquisition and pragmatics (pp. 287-307). Routledge. https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19123

### Scripted dialogues and memorized phrases ('formulaic sequences') are a plausible scaffold for beginner speaking, but controlled evidence is thin.

**Evidence:** practitioner

Nation (1995) recommends getting polite formulas, numbers and conversation-control phrases to high fluency early. Boers et al. (2006, Language Teaching Research 10(3)) is a widely cited study on teaching formulaic sequences and perceived oral proficiency; its results were not retrieved, and there is a formal call to replicate it. A later small study (Cancino & Iturrieta, 2022; 38 EFL learners) found that a Lexical Approach group beat controls on perceived oral proficiency and used more formulaic sequences. No meta-analysis was found on fading scripted dialogue into free role-play.

**For Issho:** Use a fading-scaffold sequence for each scenario: (1) listen to and shadow a full model dialogue with native audio; (2) perform it with the full script; (3) perform it from keyword cue cards; (4) perform it from the goal only, as a goal-oriented AI or couple task (finding 15). Turn the scenario's key chunks into SRS sentence cards so they get spaced retrieval. Present this to the couple as a design choice, not a proven method.

- Nation, P. (1995). Best practice in vocabulary teaching and learning. https://cunningham.acer.edu.au/inted/eaconf95/nation2.pdf

(replication call: https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/replication-research-in-pedagogical-approaches-to-spoken-fluency-and-formulaic-sequences-a-call-for-replication-of-wood-2009-and-boers-eyckmans-kappel-stengers-demecheleer-2006/8D7676F9CA677D380F6B3870CE20DA9F)

**Useful numbers**

- Oral CF meta-analysis: 33 studies; overall medium effect, maintained over time; explicit CF larger immediately, implicit more durable (Li 2010, Language Learning 60(2))
- Classroom oral CF: 15 studies, N=827; prompts > recasts, largest gap on free-production measures (Lyster & Saito 2010, SSLA 32(2))
- CF on L2 grammar: mean d = 1.16 (Russell & Spada 2006, as reported in CJAL 2022 review)
- Teachers' CF mix: recasts 57%, prompts 30% of all classroom CF (Brown 2016, LTR 20(4))
- Peer CF training: N=167 Japanese EFL university students over 1 semester; trained-CF peers gained accuracy + fluency, peer-interaction-only gained fluency only (Sato & Lyster 2012, SSLA 34(4))
- TBLT: d = 0.93 across 52 studies (Bryfonski & McKay 2019) vs re-analysis g = 0.61 (Xuan, Cheung & Liu 2025, LTR 29(7))
- Text vs face-to-face interaction: 14 studies, no significant difference in developmental benefit (Ziegler 2016, SSLA 38(3))
- Chat to speaking: 2 of 4 weekly contact hours in chat gave larger oral-proficiency gains (Payne & Whitney 2002, CALICO 20(1))
- Novice paired chat: 40-45 min sessions, 8 tasks over 1 month, 30 learners per group; only text chat reduced anxiety (Satar & Özdener 2008, MLJ 92(4))
- Anxiety-achievement correlation: r = -.36 (97 reports, N = 19,933) (Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky 2019, SSLA 41(2))
- Dialogue systems: d = 0.58 (17 studies, 100 effect sizes) (Bibauw et al. 2022, LLT 26(1))
- Dialogue systems on speaking: g = 0.61, 95% CI [0.34, 0.89] (16 studies, 89 effect sizes); goal-oriented > reactive; multimodal > voice > text (Hou & Min 2025, ReCALL)
- Generative-AI chatbots: ES ≈ 0.58 across 41 studies (JCAL 2025, doi 10.1111/jcal.70060)
- Pronunciation instruction: d = 0.89 within / 0.80 between groups, 86 reports; larger with feedback and longer interventions (Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015, Applied Linguistics 36(3))

- Task negotiation: about 6% of negotiation in communicative tasks concerned word meaning; all negotiated words came from the task sheet (Newton 1995, via Nation 1995)
- Written CF: N=268; direct and indirect comprehensive CF both improved accuracy in new texts at 1 and 4 weeks (Van Beuningen, De Jong & Kuiken 2012, LL 62(1)); WCF meta-analysis of 21 studies (Kang & Han 2015, MLJ 99(1))
- Derived target for Issho (not from any one study): 20-30 min/day solo (140-210 min/week) + 45 min/week together means together time is ~18-24% of each person's weekly minutes. To approach Nation's balance, move ~5 min/day of solo time into output (one composed sentence or a chat reply) and ~3 min/day into fluency (timed speed round), keeping FSRS and handwriting as the language-focused core

### Genki, the most widely used US university beginner text, plans about 200 class hours for 23 lessons. Each lesson gets about 6 hours of conversation/grammar and 3 hours of reading/writing. The series covers about 1,700 words and 317 kanji (145 in Genki I, 172 in Genki II), at about 15 kanji per lesson. Universities typically teach it at 4–5 class hours a week for 30–32 weeks, one volume per academic year.

**Evidence:** practitioner

The publisher says about 100 hours of study is the entry point for Genki II, so Genki I ≈ 100 class hours ≈ JLPT N5 / CEFR A1. Per class hour that works out to about 8.5 new words and about 1.6 new kanji. These are class hours. US credit norms add about 2 hours of homework per class hour (SJSU syllabus states 3 h per unit per week, 1 of them in class), so total learner time is roughly 2–3× class time. The publisher gives no count of grammar points, and I could not verify one. The 2:1 ratio of oral/grammar time to literacy time is a deliberate design choice.

**For Issho:** Use Genki densities as a pacing benchmark for the Issho curriculum. At about 3.5–4.25 h/week per person, one 'Genki-sized unit' (~15 kanji, ~70–75 words, one lesson's grammar) every 2–3 weeks matches class pacing per hour, which is roughly 5–7 kanji and 25–35 words a week. At that rate Genki I scope takes about 6–9 months. Make the literacy-vs-grammar/speaking split an explicit dial in the scheduler. Issho's handwriting emphasis will push literacy above Genki's one-third, and if nothing compensates, grammar and speaking will fall behind Genki-equivalent pace.

### Mainstream courses sequence the scripts quickly and in parallel with grammar. Genki asks learners to master hiragana during Lesson 1 and katakana during Lesson 2. Romanization disappears from Lesson 3, which is also where kanji begin. Kanji not yet taught always appear with kana readings. Each kanji list marks only some readings as 'to memorize', and vocabulary audio is recorded in both directions (Japanese→English and English→Japanese).

**Evidence:** practitioner

MIT 21G.501 (Genki I, 4 one-hour sessions/week) spent sessions 1–6 on Lesson 1 (hiragana) and 7–13 on Lesson 2 (katakana), so about 1.5–2 weeks of class per script, with grammar and conversation running alongside. Genki tells learners not to memorize kanji in vocabulary lists unless they belong to that lesson's kanji list. Kanji practice sheets come after the target kanji have been memorized.

**For Issho:** Kana phase: about 2 weeks for hiragana and about 1.5–2 weeks for katakana. Teach survival words and the first grammar points (です, question か, の) during that time instead of gating them behind 100% kana mastery. Remove romaji by about week 3–4. Introduce kanji from about week 3. Show furigana for any kanji not yet learned. Card design: teach only one or two high-value readings per kanji when it is introduced (Genki's shaded-reading approach), not the full on/kun set. For each word, generate both a recognition (JP→EN) card and a production (EN→JP) card.

### Homework and assessment in university Japanese follow a common pattern. There are frequent low-stakes vocabulary, kanji and dictation quizzes, often with the lowest scores dropped. Each lesson has a quiz or test and a workbook assignment, and many lessons end with a short composition. Oral interview tests come about twice a semester, and partner skits are performed from memory.

**Evidence:** practitioner

MIT 21G.501/21F.504: vocabulary, dictation and kanji quizzes throughout the term (two lowest dropped), 5 lesson quizzes, 2 interview tests and homework due at the start of class. SJSU JPN101C (a Genki II course): per lesson, kanji introduction → kanji quiz → vocabulary quiz → workbook due, about 2 weeks per lesson. Its partner oral quizzes require a skit of at least 8 lines per person using at least 6 of the lesson's structures. The script is submitted, corrected, rewritten, then performed without notes and graded on accuracy, fluency and creativity. Hunter College JPN 101 gives vocabulary quizzes in the first 5 minutes of most classes (search excerpt only; I could not open the PDF). Genki ends every lesson with a writing exercise.

**For Issho:** Map these patterns onto together features. (1) A 3–5 minute partner vocabulary/kanji quiz at the start of each together session. (2) A per-unit 'skit capstone': both partners co-write a dialogue that must use a checklist of the unit's structures, the AI corrects the script, then they perform it from memory and record it. (3) A dictation card type: hear a word or sentence, then handwrite it in kana or kanji. This joins listening to handwriting the way MIT's dictation quizzes do. (4) A 3–5 sentence handwritten composition at the end of each unit. (5) An 'oral interview' (run by the AI or the partner) about every 6 weeks.

### The JLPT has published no official kanji or vocabulary lists since its 2010 revision. N5 and N4 are described as reading and listening abilities. The pre-2010 specifications are still the best numeric anchor: Level 4 (now N5) ≈ 100 kanji, ≈ 800 words, 150 study hours; Level 3 (now N4) ≈ 300 kanji, ≈ 1,500 words, 300 hours. Self-reported surveys suggest learners without kanji background need about 250–400 hours for old Level 4 and about 500–750 hours for old Level 3.

**Evidence:** weak

The Japan Language Education Center data (1992–2010) are self-reported and roughly double those figures for non-kanji learners. Unofficial N5/N4 lists online vary and contain errors.

**For Issho:** Use these as Issho milestones: 'N5 scope' ≈ 100 kanji / 800 words, 'N4 scope' ≈ 300 kanji / 1,500 words. To reach N5 scope within about a year, plan for at least ~2–3 kanji and ~15–20 words a week. Genki pace (~5 kanji, ~30 words a week) is the ambitious setting. Don't treat any unofficial JLPT list as authoritative. Describe milestones as reading tasks, e.g. 'read a short notice written in kana + basic kanji'.

### The Japan Foundation's Marugoto course is built around can-do goals and topics. The Starter (A1) books each have 9 topics and 18 lessons: Katsudoo (communication) takes 30–40 h and Rikai (language competences) takes about 40 h. ### Jorden's 'Japanese: The Spoken Language' courses put speaking first and split each unit into FACT and ACT. Students learn the facts (grammar and culture explanations) from the book on their own. Add a partner 'cue → response' drill mode where the app shows one partner a cue card, the other answers aloud, and they alternate. This puts response-drill practice into the 3×15 min together sessions. Keep the speaking track on its own schedule so kanji pace never blocks grammar or speaking progress; Genki also says its conversation/grammar section can be studied on its own.

### Nation's four strands say a balanced course gives roughly equal time to four kinds of work. (1) Meaning-focused input: reading and listening to largely known material. (2) Meaning-focused output: speaking and writing to get a message across. (3) Language-focused learning: deliberate study of words, grammar, script and pronunciation. (4) Fluency development: working faster with already-known material. Nation ranks five changes as the most useful to make to a course: add extensive reading, add a fluency strand, add meaning-focused listening and speaking, provide level-appropriate listening, and train learners to use word cards.

**Evidence:** practitioner

The equal split is a heuristic. Secondary sources report that Nation called the exact division arbitrary and expected the balance to shift with proficiency; I could not open the original PDF. Each strand rests on research (input, output, explicit instruction, fluency practice), but the 25% rule itself has not been tested in a controlled study. Fluency conditions as commonly summarized: all material is familiar, attention is on meaning, there is pressure to go faster, and there is a lot of practice. Nation & Yamamoto (2012) apply the strands to self-directed learners without a teacher.

**For Issho:** Measured against the four strands, Issho's daily session (reviews → new items → handwriting → first-check → i+1 sentences) is about 75–85% language-focused learning. Rebalance over the week rather than within each session. Example 25-minute day: 10–12 min SRS, new items and handwriting; 6–8 min graded reading or listening; 3–4 min fluency (re-read yesterday's story against a timer, or a fast sprint through easy cards); 2–3 min output (say or write 2–3 sentences and send them to the partner). The 3×15 min together sessions should be mostly output and fluency. Expect language-focused learning to dominate during the kana weeks, then move toward balance.

### A meta-analysis of extensive reading (ER) found medium positive effects on reading proficiency. The effects were larger for adults and largest when ER was built into the curriculum rather than offered as an extra.

**Evidence:** strong

Jeon & Day (2016) analysed 71 samples from 49 studies (1980–2014; N = 5,919). Effect sizes: d = 0.57 for experimental vs control and d = 0.79 for pre/post. Adults: d = 0.70. ER as part of the curriculum: d = 0.91, against 0.47 when ER was only part of a reading course. Web-based stories showed larger effects than paper books, and EFL settings larger than ESL. Heterogeneity was significant. Boundary: almost all studies are of English learners. I found no comparable meta-analysis for Japanese as a second language, and beginners reading in a new script may gain less early on.

**For Issho:** Make graded reading a required part of the daily session, not an optional library visit. The analysis found the strongest effect for ER inside the curriculum, and in-app digital reading matches the 'web-based' advantage. Have both partners read the same short story each week so they can talk about it in a together session.

Sources:

- Jeon, E.-Y. & Day, R.R. (2016). The effectiveness of ER on reading proficiency: A meta-analysis. Reading in a Foreign Language, 28(2), 246–265. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/354

### Japanese graded readers (NPO Tadoku Supporters scheme) give concrete specs for beginner reading. 'Start' level assumes about 200 words of vocabulary, runs to 200 words per book, and uses only です/ます present and past, questions and 〜たい. Level 0 assumes 350 words (200–400 words per book). Level 1 (≈ N5) assumes 350 words (400–1,500 per book). Level 2 (≈ N4) assumes 500 words (1,500–3,000 per book) and adds dictionary, て and ない forms and noun modification. The books use furigana, pictures and audio.

**Evidence:** practitioner

The vocabulary bands follow the 2002 JLPT lists and grammar follows common beginner textbooks. Published series: Ask (levels 0–4), Taishukan (0–5) and NPO Tadoku books (0–3). The tadoku approach is to start very easy, read many books of one's own choosing, and read without a dictionary. A related coverage guideline is Hu & Nation (2000), widely cited for the finding that about 98% known-word coverage is needed for adequate unassisted comprehension of fiction. I confirmed its bibliographic record, but the scanned PDF could not be text-extracted here.

**For Issho:** Write or generate in-app graded stories to these specs and unlock the first 'Start' story at about 200 known words (around week 6–8). After that, move up a level by known-vocabulary count, not by the calendar. Check that each story is at or above ~95–98% known words for the learner (the app can compute this from SRS state), add furigana for unlearned kanji, attach audio for listen-and-shadow, and put new grammar in a story only after it has been taught.

Sources:

- NPO Tadoku Supporters, 'Levels Explained'. https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/levels-en/
- NPO Tadoku Supporters, 'Graded Readers'. https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/graded-readers-en/
- Hu, M. H.-c. & Nation, P. (2000). Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension. Reading in a Foreign Language, 13(1), 403–430. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/43

### Reading on its own teaches few new words. In one study, almost none of the target words a learner met fewer than 8 times while reading a graded reader were remembered three months later. Reading works mainly to consolidate and enrich words already learned, so deliberate study is still needed for new words.

**Evidence:** moderate

Waring & Takaki (2003): 15 Japanese university learners of English read a 400-headword graded reader with 25 target words (spellings changed so they would be unknown) occurring 1 to 15–18 times. Recognition decayed by more than 45% over three months. On average, the meaning of about 1 of the 25 words survived at three months, and none of the words met fewer than 8 times did. The sample is small, but the result agrees with the incidental-learning studies the authors review. The study looked at English vocabulary, not Japanese kanji.

**For Issho:** Keep FSRS as the main way new items are acquired, and use reading for consolidation and fluency. When generating i+1 sentences and graded stories, make sure each target word recurs at least 8 times across several weeks of texts. Track encounters per item and have the content generator pick under-encountered items on purpose.

Sources:

- Waring, R. & Takaki, M. (2003). At what rate do learners learn and retain new vocabulary from reading a graded reader? Reading in a Foreign Language, 15(2), 130–163. https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/78

### Explicit, focused grammar instruction produces large and lasting gains, and explicit instruction beats implicit instruction for both simple and complex grammar features. This supports the language-focused strand, including the short grammar-point lessons that university courses and Issho use.

**Evidence:** strong

Norris & Ortega (2000): 49 study samples (1980–1998). Focused L2 instruction produced large, durable gains, and explicit types beat implicit ones. Focus-on-form and focus-on-forms produced similarly large effects. Spada & Tomita (2010): 41 studies found larger effects for explicit instruction on both simple and complex features, including some measures of spontaneous use. Boundary conditions: many outcome measures favor explicit knowledge, most studies are of English L2, treatments are short, and the authors themselves flag limited generalizability.

**For Issho:** Keep short explicit grammar notes (one screen, plain English, 1–3 examples) followed right away by practice. Then have each grammar point show up in meaning-focused use: i+1 sentences, the chat-tip feature, role-plays. This counters the pure-immersion, no-grammar approach of some self-study communities (AJATT/Refold) for complete beginners on a limited time budget.

Sources:

- Norris, J.M. & Ortega, L. (2000). Effectiveness of L2 instruction: A research synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis. Language Learning, 50(3), 417–528. https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136
- Spada, N. & Tomita, Y. (2010). Interactions between type of instruction and type of language feature: A meta-analysis. Language Learning, 60(2), 263–308. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x

### Repeated, time-pressured retelling of the same content (the 4/3/2 technique: the same talk given three times in 4, 3 and 2 minutes) raises speaking speed and cuts hesitations. In one study the gains lasted only when the topic was repeated.

**Evidence:** moderate

Arevart & Nation (1991): learners retold the same story three times to different listeners with less time each round, and they spoke faster with fewer hesitations, above their usual fluency level. De Jong & Perfetti (2011): 24 ESL students gave 4/3/2 speeches. Both the same-topic and different-topic groups improved during training, but only the same-topic group kept the gains on posttests, which the authors explain as proceduralization. The samples are small, the learners were intermediate English learners, and I found no beginner-Japanese replication.

**For Issho:** Add a fluency game to the together sessions. Each partner tells the same short content three times with shrinking time limits, scaled for beginners to about 90 / 60 / 45 seconds. The first telling can go to the AI tutor, the second to the spouse, and the third is recorded. Repeat the same topic ('my day', 'what I ate') across the week before changing it. This fills the fluency strand that Issho currently lacks.

Sources:

- Arevart, S. & Nation, P. (1991). Fluency improvement in a second language. RELC Journal, 22(1), 84–94. https://doi.org/10.1177/003368829102200106
- de Jong, N. & Perfetti, C.A. (2011). Fluency training in the ESL classroom: An experimental study of fluency development and proceduralization. Language Learning, 61(2), 533–568. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00620.x

### Shadowing (repeating audio aloud almost at the same time as the speaker) has some evidence for improving phoneme perception and, for lower-proficiency learners, listening comprehension. The evidence comes from Japanese learners of English, not English speakers learning Japanese.

**Evidence:** weak

Hamada (2016): 43 Japanese university EFL learners, 9 shadowing lessons. Phoneme perception (dictation cloze) improved in both the low- and intermediate-proficiency groups, but listening comprehension improved only in the low group. There was a single site and no strong control. Studies in Japan widely claim shadowing works, but the author notes the evidence behind those claims is thin.

**For Issho:** Keep listen/shadow in the daily session, since it is cheap and may help most at the absolute-beginner stage. Keep it short (1–3 sentences), shadow with the text visible first and then without, and don't count it as meaning-focused input. Pair it with pitch-accent display if the app supports that. Treat it as a pronunciation and listening aid, not proof that speaking has been mastered.

Sources:

- Hamada, Y. (2016). Shadowing: Who benefits and how? Uncovering a booming EFL teaching technique for listening comprehension. Language Teaching Research, 20(1), 35–52. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815597504

### Teachers of Japanese most often teach kanji through rote writing. Teachers who value kanji's cultural and practical role use more memory strategies (mnemonics, components) and more context strategies. Japan's own schools have children read each grade's kanji that year but write them only from the following year: 80 kanji in grade 1, 160 in grade 2, 1,026 by the end of grade 6.

**Evidence:** practitioner

Shimizu & Green (2002) surveyed 251 teachers of Japanese and identified three strategy types (context, memory, rote). Rote was the most frequent; memory and context strategies went with positive attitudes toward kanji. The survey describes what teachers do, not which strategy works best. The school read-now/write-later rule is as described on Japanese Wikipedia, which attributes it to the 2017 Course of Study; I could not check the MEXT text itself. Grade allocations follow the 2017 revision (in force from 2020).

**For Issho:** (1) Build a 'write lag' into scheduling. Recognition cards for a kanji start when it is introduced. Write-from-memory production cards should be scheduled again only once recognition is stable, or for a chosen high-frequency subset, so that handwriting reviews do not swamp the 25-minute budget. (2) Keep the 3-pass handwriting for introduction, but always pair it with component and radical mnemonics and 1–3 real words or a sentence in context (memory and context strategies), never 'write it 10 times' alone. (3) Pace check: Japanese children learn 80–200 kanji a year with daily school practice, and Genki covers about 145 a year, so about 3–5 new kanji a week is a sustainable range for adults studying part time.

Sources:

- Shimizu, H. & Green, K.E. (2002). Japanese language educators' strategies for and attitudes toward teaching kanji. The Modern Language Journal, 86(2), 227–241. https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-4781.00146
- Wikipedia (ja), 学年別漢字配当表 (Grade-allocated kanji table; reading vs. writing expectations). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/学年別漢字配当表
- Wikipedia, 'Kyōiku kanji' (per-grade counts, 1,026 total). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%8Diku_kanji

### Popular SRS-based self-study paths are much faster than classroom pace only because they drop handwriting and use far more time. WaniKani sequences radicals → kanji → vocabulary with mnemonics and SRS and claims 2,000 kanji and 6,000 words in 'just over a year' (elsewhere 'a year and a half'), all recognition only. The Anki manual notes that 20 new cards a day leads to roughly 200 reviews a day, and its FSRS default desired retention is 90%.

**Evidence:** practitioner

WaniKani's pace claims come from its marketing page and assume heavy daily use. It teaches no handwriting, and it orders kanji by component structure rather than by textbook or frequency. The Anki 10:1 review-to-new ratio is the manual's example, not a fixed rule, and FSRS changes the ratio. No controlled studies compare these paths with classroom courses.

**For Issho:** Issho's radical → kanji → word chain matches WaniKani's structure, which is good. Its pace is not reachable with 3-pass handwriting in 20–30 minutes. Cap new items per person at about 5–8 a day across all types (≈1 kanji with words, 3–5 vocab or survival words, 2–4 grammar points a week). Use FSRS's default 90% retention, and let the scheduler throttle new items whenever the projected review load passes about 10–12 minutes. For 'as fast as possible', raise the cap only on days with extra time, not by default.

Sources:

### Self-study communities disagree with textbooks on two sequencing questions. Tae Kim's guide teaches plain (dictionary) forms before polite です/ます forms and leans heavily on kanji, while Genki and most courses start with polite forms. Refold's roadmap puts comprehension and listening before a dedicated speaking phase, while every university syllabus reviewed here includes speaking from week 1.

**Evidence:** practitioner

Tae Kim argues that conjugation is built on the dictionary form, so learning polite forms first is backwards. Refold's phases run Immersion → Foundations → Comprehension → Listening → Speaking → Accuracy → Fluency. Within this lens I found no controlled evidence settling either question for adult beginners. Classroom practice (MIT drill sessions, oral interview tests, skits) assumes early output.

**For Issho:** (1) Register: husband and wife will naturally use casual speech with each other. Teach plain-form recognition, and simple casual patterns for the spouse chat, earlier than Genki does, while keeping です/ます for survival phrases and role-plays with strangers (shop clerk, hotel). Tag each grammar card and chat tip with its register. (2) Don't delay output. The couple's motivation centers on talking together, and classroom models speak from day 1. Borrow Refold's strength instead: an optional 'immersion log' (listening or reading outside the app) that counts toward meaning-focused input hours.

## Adherence, motivation and habit formation for adult self-study apps (habit timelines, implementation intentions, streaks and loss aversion, self-determination theory and couples, progress and milestones, partner effects, optimal challenge, SRS review debt, micro-sessions, motivation decay and the plateau). ### A complex daily routine like a 20–30 minute study session takes months to become automatic, not 21 or 30 days. The earliest repetitions add the most automaticity, and missing a single day does not materially set it back.

**Evidence:** moderate

Lally et al. (2010) had 96 adults repeat one behavior daily in the same context. The model fit well for only 39 people. Their time to reach 95% of peak automaticity averaged 66 days and ranged from 18 to 254. Missing one opportunity barely affected the curve, while longer breaks did. A 2024 systematic review (Singh et al.; 20 studies, N=2,601, mostly high risk of bias) found only 4 studies reporting timing: medians of 59–66 days, means of 106–154 days, and a range of 4–335. Simple behaviors such as flossing formed faster than complex ones such as diet. Buyalskaya et al. (2023, PNAS) used machine learning on more than 12M gym check-ins and more than 40M handwashing events. Handwashing became predictable within weeks, but gym habits took months (roughly 4–7 months median). Boundary conditions: all of this is health behavior, and the automaticity measures are self-report (Lally) or predictability-based (Buyalskaya). No study covers language-study habits directly. A multi-step study session is closer to going to the gym than to washing hands.

**For Issho:** Treat the first ~12–16 weeks as a dedicated 'foundation season' whose main goal is the habit, not volume. Don't announce 'habit formed' at day 21 or 30. Keep the daily cue and slot fixed through month 4 at least. Frame a single missed day as harmless, because the evidence says it is. Spend design effort on stopping 2+ day gaps rather than on keeping a perfect 7/7 record.

Sources:

- Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 ; summary: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit ; critique: https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit
- Singh, B., Murphy, A., Maher, C., & Smith, A. E. (2024). Time to form a habit: A systematic review and meta-analysis of health behaviour habit formation and its determinants. Healthcare, 12(23), 2488. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11641623/
- Buyalskaya, A., Ho, H., Milkman, K. L., Li, X., Duckworth, A. L., & Camerer, C. (2023). What can machine learning teach us about habit formation? Evidence from exercise and hygiene. PNAS, 120(17), e2216115120. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500

### The best predictor of whether someone does the behavior today is how long it has been since they last did it. Frequent, consistent repetition (about 4+ days a week) is needed for the habit to take hold.

**Evidence:** moderate

In Buyalskaya et al. (2023), days since the last visit was the strongest predictor of gym attendance. A longer gap lowered the chance of attending for 76% of gym-goers. Attendance in the past 7 days (positive for 82%) and same-weekday streaks (69%) were also strong. Time of day was not a key predictor for the gym. Kaushal & Rhodes (2015; 111 new gym members over 12 weeks) concluded that at least 4 sessions a week for 6 weeks was the minimum to establish an exercise habit, and that consistency and cues drove habit formation. Boundary conditions: this is exercise data, not language study, and it is observational.

**For Issho:** Track 'days since last session' per person as the main adherence metric, internally and gently in the UI. After 1 missed day, the next open should offer a frictionless 2–3 minute re-entry session: only the most-overdue reviews, no new items, no handwriting. Set the weekly target at 5 of 7 days, with ≥4 as the minimum that still counts as habit-building, rather than demanding 7/7.

Sources:

- Buyalskaya, A., et al. (2023). PNAS, 120(17), e2216115120. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500
- Kaushal, N., & Rhodes, R. E. (2015). Exercise habit formation in new gym members: A longitudinal study. Journal of Behavioral Medicine, 38, 652–663. https://www.uvic.ca/research/labs/bmed/assets/docs/Kaushal,%20Rhodes,%202015.pdf

### If-then plans ('when situation X happens, I will do Y') reliably raise goal attainment by a medium-to-large amount.

**Evidence:** strong

Gollwitzer & Sheeran's (2006) meta-analysis pooled 94 independent tests (more than 8,000 participants) and found d ≈ 0.65 (95% CI ~0.6–0.7). The benefit appeared at several stages: getting started, shielding an ongoing activity from distraction, and disengaging from failing strategies. Secondary sources say the effect survived a publication-bias correction. Habit research (Wood & Neal 2007) also stresses that habits attach to stable context cues (place, the preceding action) rather than to goals. Boundary conditions: most studies are health and academic tasks with short follow-up. d is a standardized difference, not a percentage gain.

**For Issho:** During onboarding, each spouse writes a personal if-then plan tied to an existing routine, e.g. 'After I pour my morning coffee at the kitchen table, I open Issho and do my reviews.' They also write a coping plan: 'If I miss the morning slot, I do the 3-minute review before brushing my teeth.' The couple writes a joint plan for the 3 weekly together sessions (e.g. 'Tue/Thu/Sun right after dinner, on the couch'). Reminders fire at each person's chosen cue time and quote their own plan back to them. Prompt a review of the plans at weeks 4 and 8.

Sources:

- Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 ; summary with CI: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/pdf
- Wood, W., & Neal, D. T. (2007). A new look at habits and the habit–goal interface. Psychological Review, 114(4), 843–863. https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.neal_.2007psychrev_a_new_look_at_habits_and_the_interface_between_habits_and_goals.pdf

### Streaks help while they are intact, but a broken streak, especially one people blame on themselves and that the app highlights, lowers later engagement. Letting people repair a streak softens the damage. A streak tied to a demanding daily goal backfires.

**Evidence:** moderate

Silverman & Barasch (2023, J Consumer Research, 7 studies): showing a streak as intact in an activity log raised later engagement compared with showing it as broken. The effect depended on how the log looked, not on what people actually did. It was stronger when people blamed the break on themselves and weaker when they could repair the streak. GitHub silently removed its streak counters (Moldon, Strohmaier & Wachs, ICSE 2021). Long streaks and weekend activity then dropped, and some people pursuing public 100-day goals gave up. Duolingo's company A/B results (practitioner): letting one lesson extend the streak, instead of requiring the full daily XP goal, raised 7+ day streaks by more than 40%, Day-14 retention by 3.3% relative, and daily active users by 1%. Learners with an 'intense' daily goal had been the least likely to keep a streak. Harm: the streak becomes a goal in its own right and can crowd out the real one (secondary report of a field study with the one sec app).

**For Issho:** A streak day should count when the minimum is done (today's due reviews cleared, or one 3-minute micro-session), not the full 25-minute plan. Never show a broken streak prominently. Lead with 'days studied this month' and 'longest streak', and show the current streak only while it is alive. Let people restore a streak with a make-up session within 48 hours. Offer a 'travel/sick' mode so legitimate breaks count as external causes. Keep the full daily plan as a separate, optional 'goal met' badge.

Sources:

- Silverman, J., & Barasch, A. (2023). On or off track: How (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions. Journal of Consumer Research, 49(6), 1095–1117. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029 ; accepted manuscript: https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb
- Moldon, L., Strohmaier, M., & Wachs, J. (2021). How gamification affects software developers: Cautionary evidence from a natural experiment on GitHub. ICSE 2021. https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02371

### Building a small, limited allowance of skips into a demanding daily goal ('emergency reserves') beats both an easy goal and a strict 7/7 goal. It also makes people more likely to bounce back the day after a miss.

**Evidence:** moderate

In Sharif & Shu's field experiment, 273 university students and staff were randomized after a baseline week, each with a personal step goal of 120% of baseline, for 4 weeks. Conditions were Easy (5 days/week), Hard (7/7), Reserve-Weekly (7/7 with 2 skips per week that don't roll over), and Reserve-Monthly (8 skips to use anytime). Reserve groups hit their goal on about 4.0 (weekly) and 3.8 (monthly) days a week, versus 2.83 for Hard and 3.11 for Easy. After a failed day where a skip was used, 55% succeeded the next day, versus 37% in Hard. Reserve groups also took more steps per day. The flexible monthly reserve helped only people high in self-control, while the weekly reserve helped everyone. The published OBHDP version adds lab studies showing a greater sense of progress and commitment. Duolingo practitioner data agrees: allowing two equipped Streak Freezes raised daily active learners by 0.38%, and a 'Weekend Amulet' made learners 4% more likely to return a week later and 5% less likely to lose their streak. Boundary conditions: step counts, not studying, over 1 month.

**For Issho:** Make the default weekly plan '7 days with 2 rest tokens per week, no rollover'. Applying a token must be a deliberate tap so it carries a small psychological cost. A token used on a missed day keeps the streak and the couple quest alive. Pre-schedule an 'easy day' option (e.g. Sunday = reviews only, ~5 minutes). Use weekly tokens, not a monthly pool, because they helped regardless of self-control. Give the couple's shared streak its own small reserve.

### Rewarding the comeback after a miss was the best of 54 nudges in a 61,293-person randomized megastudy. But most nudges stop working once they end, so gamified rewards cannot replace a real habit and a cue.

**Evidence:** strong

Milkman et al. (2021, Nature) tested 54 four-week digital programs on gym members of a US fitness chain. About 45% of programs raised weekly visits, by roughly 9–27%. The top performer gave small rewards (about 20 cents in points) for coming back after a missed workout. Only about 8% of programs still had a measurable effect after the 4 weeks. Expert forecasters could not predict which programs would work. Boundary conditions: gym attendance with monetary-equivalent points, not language study.

**For Issho:** Make the session after a gap a celebrated 'comeback': warm copy, a small bonus, and an optional automatic 'welcome back' to the spouse. Never open with 'You missed 3 days.' Because nudge effects fade, put the main effort into the cue, the if-then plan and a low-friction minimum session, and treat badges and points as seasoning. Run the app as a personal megastudy: log which features keep each spouse coming back and adjust.

### Spaced-repetition burnout comes from accumulated review debt: every new card creates future reviews, and missed days pile up. The target retention rate is the biggest lever on the daily workload.

**Evidence:** practitioner

The FSRS documentation says workload plotted against desired retention is U-shaped. Higher retention shortens intervals, while lower retention causes more forgetting and relearning. Since Anki 24.04 the optimizer minimizes workload per unit of knowledge. FSRS calculators (RemNote) estimate that 95% retention gives intervals about half as long as 90% (roughly double the reviews), and 97% gives about 0.29× the interval (about 3.5× the reviews). Anki 24.11 added a Load Balancer (evens out due counts within the interval fuzz range) and Easy Days (lighter or no reviews on chosen weekdays). Community and practitioner reports describe a 'death spiral': a missed day doubles the next day's load, and 15 new cards a day can mean 100–150+ daily reviews within a month. These are rules of thumb that depend on settings. No controlled study of SRS dropout was found.

**For Issho:** Schedule from the time budget. Each person sets daily minutes (e.g. 20). Reviews go first, and new items are unlocked only if the projected 7- and 30-day review load stays within budget (design heuristic: keep projected reviews at or below about 60–70% of the budget so there is room for new learning and reading). Default desired retention to about 0.88–0.90, never 0.95+. Turn on load balancing and let each person mark one 'easy day' a week. Backlog recovery mode: when overdue reviews exceed one day's budget, pause new items automatically, prioritize by lowest retrievability and highest value (e.g. kanji that unlock planned readings), and spread the rest over 3–5 days. Show time ('~17 min today'), never a scary card count.

### In simple models, learning is fastest when the learner is right about 85% of the time. That points to a sweet spot of difficulty, not to maximum ease.

**Evidence:** weak

Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019, Nature Communications) showed mathematically that for gradient-descent learners on binary classification, the optimal training error rate is about 15.9% (≈85% accuracy). Simulations of neural networks and an animal-learning model matched this. The lead author warned against carrying the exact number into classrooms; the point is that a sweet spot exists. It happens to sit close to the common FSRS retention default (0.85–0.90 recall). Boundary conditions: theory and simulation on two-choice tasks, not human vocabulary or kanji learning.

**For Issho:** Use about 80–90% first-try success as a guide for each session, not a hard rule. If first-check retrieval drops below about 75%, cut new items in the next session and add easier recognition work. If it stays above about 95% for a week, offer more new items or harder i+1 sentences. In the 3-pass handwriting flow, offer stroke hints on the memory pass so success stays in the sweet spot rather than at zero.

Sources:

- Wilson, R. C., Shenhav, A., Straccia, M., & Cohen, J. D. (2019). The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning. Nature Communications, 10, 4646. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579
- Psychology Today (2019). The right level of struggle for learning (author caveats). https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-waves/201911/the-right-level-of-struggle-for-learning

### Self-chosen ('autonomous') motivation goes with better L2 achievement and less anxiety, while pressure-based ('controlled') motivation does not help. Partners help most by understanding and encouraging, not by directing.

**Evidence:** moderate

Alamer, Saeedy Robat, Elahi Shirvan & Ryan (2025, Educational Psychology Review) ran a multilevel meta-analysis of SDT studies in language learning. Intrinsic regulation correlated with L2 achievement at r = .26, identified regulation at .16, and autonomous motivation overall at .23. Introjected (guilt or pride) and external regulation were unrelated to achievement (overall controlled r = −.03), and controlled motivation correlated with more anxiety (r = .16). The data are correlational. Koestner et al. (2012, PSPB; 3 prospective studies over ~3 months, one with romantic partners) found that autonomy support, meaning empathic perspective-taking, predicted goal progress through stronger autonomous motivation, and also predicted relationship quality and well-being. Directive support, meaning guidance and pushing, was only marginally linked to progress and unrelated to relationship quality.

**For Issho:** Give real choices every day: which story to read, the role-play scenario, whether to do handwriting now or later, and explain why each step exists. Avoid guilt-based copy and loss framing; streak warnings should not shame. Partner features should be supportive: send a stamp or a voice note in Japanese, 'saw you did your kanji', or celebrate a partner's milestone. Don't build surveillance: no default alert that 'your partner hasn't studied', and each person decides what the other can see. Let both partners set their own pace and goals within the shared plan.

### A vivid picture of your future self using the language strongly predicts how much effort you intend to put in, but only weakly predicts what you actually achieve.

**Evidence:** strong

Al-Hoorie (2018, SSLLT) meta-analyzed 32 reports (39 samples, N = 32,078). The ideal L2 self correlated with intended effort at r = .61 but with objective achievement at only about r = .20. The ought-to self (others' expectations) was unrelated to achievement (r ≈ −.05). A larger dissertation meta-analysis (100 studies) found the same pattern: r = .61 for effort and .24 for achievement. Heterogeneity was substantial, and the measures are self-report.

**For Issho:** At onboarding, build a concrete shared vision, e.g. 'read the menu and order together at a Kyoto izakaya in spring 2027', with a target date and a photo, and show it again at weekly check-ins. Don't confuse feeling motivated with progress: link the vision to concrete can-do milestones and to measured retrieval and reading, and drop 'should' framings (others' expectations), which don't help.

Sources:

- Al-Hoorie, A. H. (2018). The L2 motivational self system: A meta-analysis. Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching, 8(4), 721–754. https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/12295

### Confidence in your own ability (self-efficacy) is one of the strongest correlates of L2 achievement, and it tends to erode during the first year of study, especially for speaking and listening.

**Evidence:** moderate

Goetze & Driver (2022, SSLLT) meta-analyzed 37 studies (40 samples, N = 23,050) and found self-efficacy correlated with achievement at r = .46. The size varied by first language, target language and proficiency level, and the data are cross-sectional, so cause and effect may run both ways. Bandura's theory names mastery experience (doing it successfully yourself) as the strongest source of self-efficacy. Busse (2013, System) followed 59 first-year university learners of German. Their self-efficacy for speaking and listening declined over the year, and intrinsic motivation fell alongside it (Busse & Walter 2013, MLJ).

**For Issho:** Plan frequent, real mastery moments. Each week, show a real-world artifact (sign, menu, station name, manga panel) built only from items the learner knows, with 'You can read 8 of these 9 characters.' In spoken role-plays, start fully scaffolded (script, then hints, then free) so speaking confidence doesn't collapse. Watch for falling self-ratings around months 2–4 and respond with easier wins, not more volume.

Sources:

- Goetze, J., & Driver, M. (2022). Is learning really just believing? A meta-analysis of self-efficacy and achievement in SLA. Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching, 12(2). https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/34236
- Busse, V. (2013). An exploration of motivation and self-beliefs of first year students of German. System, 41(2), 379–398. https://cris.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/60459106
- Busse, V., & Walter, C. (2013). Foreign language learning motivation in higher education: A longitudinal study of motivational changes and their causes. The Modern Language Journal, 97(2), 435–456. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.12004.x

### Tracking your progress improves goal attainment, and the effect is bigger when the progress is reported to someone or written down physically.

**Evidence:** strong

Harkin et al. (2016, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analyzed 138 randomized studies (N = 19,951). Prompting people to monitor progress greatly increased monitoring (d = 1.98) and improved goal attainment (d = 0.40). How much more people monitored explained part of that effect. The effect was larger when outcomes were reported or made public and when the information was physically recorded. Boundary conditions: mostly health goals. Subgroup estimates from secondary sources (public d ≈ .55 vs private unreported d ≈ .19) rest on few studies and were not verified against the paper.

**For Issho:** Add a 5-minute weekly 'couple check-in' screen. Each spouse records what they learned (kanji, words, grammar), their sessions and one can-do statement, then shares it with the other. The record is kept and viewable as history. Offer an optional handwritten kanji journal page that they photograph and attach, which also counts as handwriting practice. Sharing is reporting to the partner, so it should stay supportive (see the autonomy-support finding).

Sources:

- Harkin, B., Webb, T. L., Chang, B. P. I., Prestwich, A., Conner, M., Kellar, I., Benn, Y., & Sheeran, P. (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? Psychological Bulletin, 142(2), 198–229. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025 ; open copy: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/ ; APA release: https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals

### How progress is displayed changes effort. People speed up near a goal, feel motivated by whichever is smaller (work done vs work left), and sag in the middle of a long goal.

**Evidence:** moderate

Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006, JMR), from a café loyalty card and an online song-rating program: customers bought more often as they neared the reward. A 12-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled was completed faster than a 10-stamp card (illusory head start). Koo & Fishbach (2012, JCR): early on, highlighting what's done ('20% complete') motivates more than what's left. Near the end, highlighting what's left ('20% to go') works better, because each step feels bigger against the smaller number. Bonezzi, Brendl & De Angelis (2011, Psych Science): motivation dips around the halfway point because both start and finish feel far away. Boundary conditions: mostly loyalty programs and short lab tasks.

**For Issho:** Don't show one giant progress bar like '312 of 2,136 jōyō kanji', which would leave the learner in the middle for years. Split the path into short chapters: sets of 10–20 kanji, 4-week seasons, one-month 'can-do' quests. In the first half of a chapter show what's done; in the second half show what's left ('3 kanji to finish Set 7'). Give a legitimate head start (e.g. count completed kana and radicals toward chapter 1). Put the next milestone ahead of the middle-dip point.

Sources:

- Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). The goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected: Purchase acceleration, illusionary goal progress, and customer retention. Journal of Marketing Research, 43(1), 39–58. https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf
- Koo, M., & Fishbach, A. (2012). The small-area hypothesis: Effects of progress monitoring on goal adherence. Journal of Consumer Research, 39(3), 493–509. https://doi.org/10.1086/663827 ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-663827.html
- Bonezzi, A., Brendl, C. M., & De Angelis, M. (2011). Stuck in the middle: The psychophysics of goal pursuit. Psychological Science, 22(5), 607–612. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611404899 ; https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2011/stuck-in-the-middle-the-psychophysics-of-goal-pursuit

### Can-do statements are reliable for low-stakes progress tracking, but learners' self-ratings match tested proficiency only moderately, and accuracy depends on level and experience.

**Evidence:** moderate

Tigchelaar (MSU dissertation; Rasch analysis of 50 NCSSFL-ACTFL spoken can-do statements, 382 Spanish learners): item difficulties broadly followed the predicted ACTFL levels at the major thresholds. Brown, Dewey & Cox (2014, Russian study abroad): self-assessment was highly reliable and showed significant gains, but correlated only moderately with OPI scores. Studies of L2 Chinese learners found that self-ratings tracked gains, learners tended to underrate themselves, and intermediate learners were the least accurate. Less-experienced speakers tend to overrate themselves. A 2023 review in Language Teaching reports wide variation in self-assessment accuracy depending on item wording and format.

**For Issho:** Use a can-do ladder (e.g. 'I can read station names', 'I can order food', 'I can write my partner a 3-sentence note') as the motivating map for each season. Check each can-do with a 1–2 minute performance task (read this real menu; the app checks comprehension) before awarding it. That gives mastery evidence (see the self-efficacy finding) and corrects over- or under-confidence. Never use self-ratings alone to set SRS difficulty.

Sources:

- Tigchelaar, M. Assessing the validity of ACTFL Can-Do Statements for spoken proficiency (Michigan State University dissertation). https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/19265
- Brown, N. A., Dewey, D. P., & Cox, T. L. (2014). Language Testing (can-do self-assessment validity, Russian study abroad). https://doi.org/10.1177/0265532214541885 ; https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/5880
- Self-assessment in second language learning. Language Teaching (2023, Cambridge). https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/00D73BD54F6227D484297EEAB8F51BC0/S0261444822000489a.pdf/self-assessment-in-second-language-learning.pdf

### Natural 'new beginnings' (a new week, month or semester, a birthday) increase aspirational behavior. That makes them good moments to restart after a lapse or to begin a new phase.

**Evidence:** moderate

Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014, Management Science), three archival field studies: Google searches for 'diet', university gym visits and goal-commitment sign-ups all rose after temporal landmarks, including the start of a week, month or semester, after a birthday, and after holidays. Boundary conditions: observational data, and the boost to starting a behavior doesn't guarantee it lasts.

**For Issho:** Start new chapters and seasons on Mondays or the 1st of the month. After a lapse of 3 or more days, offer a 'fresh start' at the next landmark ('New week. Start Season 3 together on Monday?') with a rebuilt, smaller review queue, not a list of missed days. Schedule couple goal-setting for the first together session of each month.

Sources:

- Dai, H., Milkman, K. L., & Riis, J. (2014). The fresh start effect: Temporal landmarks motivate aspirational behavior. ### A partner raises persistence most when the task is joint (the team result depends on the weaker member) and the partner is only moderately better. Gamification works best when collaboration and competition are combined, though its effects are small.

**Evidence:** moderate

Köhler effect: Feltz, Irwin & Kerr (2012; N = 135, randomized exergame) used a virtual partner on a joint task, where the team score ended when the first partner quit. Persistence rose by about 34 seconds across partner conditions, while solo controls fell by about 49 seconds. A partner about 40% better produced the largest gain (+51 s), compared with +23 s (1% better) and +27 s (100% better), an inverted-U pattern. Sailer & Homner (2020) meta-analyzed gamification in learning: cognitive g = .49, motivational g = .36, behavioral g = .25 (behavioral and motivational estimates less stable). Game fiction and combining competition with collaboration were particularly effective for behavioral outcomes. Duolingo (practitioner, apparently observational): learners with at least one Friend Streak were 22% more likely to complete their daily lesson. Boundary conditions: single-session lab exercise, school and university gamification studies, and no controlled study of couples learning a language together was found.

**For Issho:** Make the core together mechanic a shared goal: a 'couple streak' or weekly couple quest counts only when both spouses do at least their minimum, with its own small reserve (see the emergency-reserve finding). Add light, friendly competition inside it, such as 'who taught the other a new word', quiz duels in the 15-minute together sessions, and a co-op score made of both contributions. Compare effort and consistency (sessions, minutes), not raw volume. If one spouse is far ahead, frame the gap as small, put the stronger one in a teaching role (they quiz, they model the role-play), and keep new-item pacing within a moderate gap so the slower partner is motivated, not discouraged.

Sources:

- Feltz, D. L., Irwin, B., & Kerr, N. (2012). Two-player partnered exergame for obesity prevention: Using discrepancy in players' abilities as a strategy to motivate physical activity. Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology, 6(4), 820–827. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3440153
- Sailer, M., & Homner, L. (2020). The gamification of learning: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 32(1), 77–112. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w ; open: https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056
- Shuttleworth, J. (2024, Sep 20). 5 product lessons we learned from building Friend Streak. Duolingo Blog (practitioner). https://blog.duolingo.com/product-lessons-friend-streak/

### Reminder notifications cause short-term study sessions, but the same message wears out with repetition and regains its effect after a rest. Small, varied, well-timed reminders help modestly.

**Evidence:** moderate

Yancey & Settles (KDD 2020) built a 'sleeping, recovering' bandit for Duolingo practice reminders. It accounts for novelty effects (templates lose power with repeated use and recover after a break) and for messages that are only valid in certain situations. Over a strong baseline it raised daily active users by 0.5% and new-user retention by 2%, measured on a dataset of 200M reminders. Bell et al. (2023, JMIR mHealth; Drink Less micro-randomized trial, n = 350): a notification raised the odds of opening the app within the next hour about 3.5-fold, with no significant decline over the study. Duolingo practitioner reports (via Econsultancy) say re-engagement messages sent about 23.5 hours after the last lesson worked best. That is an unpublished company result.

**For Issho:** Send one reminder a day per person, at their if-then cue time, or about 23.5 hours after the last session if no cue time is set. Rotate a varied set of messages, including Japanese micro-content such as the kanji of the day, and don't reuse a message for several days. Keep messages free of guilt. Let a spouse send a personal nudge only occasionally, since it is the most novel channel. If someone ignores reminders for about 5 days, stop the daily ones and switch to a weekly fresh-start message so reminders don't train them to ignore the app.

Sources:

- Yancey, K. P., & Settles, B. (2020). A sleeping, recovering bandit algorithm for optimizing recurring notifications. KDD '20. https://research.duolingo.com/papers/yancey.kdd20.pdf ; https://paperswithcode.com/paper/a-sleeping-recovering-bandit-algorithm-for
- Bell, L., et al. (2023). Effect of notifications on engagement with a behavior change app: micro-randomized trial (Drink Less). JMIR mHealth and uHealth, 11, e38342. https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342/PDF
- Econsultancy. Six A/B tests used by Duolingo to tap into habit-forming behaviour (secondary/practitioner). https://econsultancy.com/six-a-b-tests-used-by-duolingo-to-tap-into-habit-forming-behaviour/

### Busy adults fit language practice into short phone sessions spread through the day. Persistence is a cycle of engagement, breaks and returns, not a straight line. Splitting the daily load and designing for re-entry suits these learners better than one rigid block.

**Evidence:** weak

Dingler et al. (MobileHCI 2017; controlled plus in-the-wild study): notification-triggered vocabulary prompts produced many short 'quick' sessions, and 37.6% of sessions happened in transit. Cai et al. (CHI 2015, WaitChatter; 20 people, 2 weeks) found that learners picked up about 57 new words each from micro-quizzes shown while waiting for chat replies. Users were most receptive right after sending a message, and the authors note it suits vocabulary rather than complex skills. Hwang, Coss, Loewen & Tagarelli (2024, SSLA; 3,670 adult app users, survival analysis of usage logs) describe persistence as cycles of engagement, disengagement, dormancy and re-engagement. Learners who accepted the app more took more pauses but had longer active stretches, shorter breaks and fewer dropouts. Spacing study across the day is also supported by distributed-practice research (Cepeda et al. 2006).

**For Issho:** Split each person's daily 20–30 minutes into (a) one or two 3–8 minute 'anywhere' bursts (SRS recognition reviews, listening and shadowing, no pen needed), usable on a commute or while waiting, and (b) one 12–20 minute 'desk' block for new items and the trace, guided and memory handwriting passes. Allow the review queue to be done in pieces across the day. Put the together features at natural wait times: suggest a quick Japanese tip during the couple chat. Expect dormant weeks and build a smooth re-entry path (see the comeback and fresh-start findings) rather than counting them as failure.

Sources:

- Dingler, T., Weber, D., Pielot, M., Cooper, J., Chang, C.-C., & Henze, N. (2017). Language learning on-the-go: Opportune moments and design of mobile microlearning sessions. MobileHCI '17. https://doi.org/10.1145/3098279.3098565 ; https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3098279.3098565
- Cai, C. J., Guo, P. J., Glass, J. R., & Miller, R. C. (2015). Wait-learning: Leveraging wait time for second language education. CHI 2015. https://sls.csail.mit.edu/archives/root/publications/2015/Cai_CHI-2015.pdf ; https://news.mit.edu/2015/learn-language-while-you-text-0514
- Hwang, H.-B., Coss, M. D., Loewen, S., & Tagarelli, K. M. (2024). Acceptance and engagement patterns of mobile-assisted language learning among non-conventional adult L2 learners: A survival analysis. Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 46(4), 969–995. https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/85DDA2047A7A04BA7FE80541433595C6
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354

### Motivation rises and falls within individuals, settling into stable phases that depend on context. The commonly cited month 2–3 slump and 'intermediate plateau' are mostly practitioner ideas without solid evidence, but realistic expectations matter for Japanese, which takes many hundreds of hours.

**Evidence:** weak

Waninge, Dörnyei & de Bot (2014, MLJ; 4 learners tracked in fine detail over 2 weeks) found that motivation changes over time yet has predictable stable phases and cannot be separated from context. Longitudinal studies (Busse 2013) show declines in intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy over a first year. No peer-reviewed curve of month 2–3 decay in language apps was found. Duolingo-style claims such as 28% active at day 30 come from unverified sources and were not used. The 'intermediate plateau' (around CEFR B1) appears only in practitioner and advocacy writing (e.g. ESL Pod) and small qualitative teacher studies, with no controlled evidence. The Foreign Service Institute places Japanese in its hardest category: about 88 weeks / 2,200 class hours to professional working proficiency (ILR 3) for experienced adult learners in full-time classes with 3–4 hours of daily self-study. This comes from secondary sources, because the State Department page could not be fetched.

**For Issho:** Set honest expectations at onboarding. At about 25 minutes a day plus 45 minutes together a week, each spouse logs roughly 190 hours a year, so the realistic goals are a seasonal series (kana, then survival reading, then N5-level reading, then N4) rather than 'fluency'. Show cumulative hours alongside the FSI-style long-term map. Plan new, authentic content for weeks 6–12, the expected post-novelty dip: the first graded reader, the first full unscripted role-play, the first handwritten note to the spouse. Treat month 3 as a scheduled 'season finale' with a can-do showcase. After the beginner stage, switch progress tracking from item counts (which slow down) to reading-comprehension milestones, so slowing word counts don't feel like a plateau.
